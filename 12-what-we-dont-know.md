# What We Don't Know

*Acting well under genuine uncertainty.*

Two of the most respected economic institutions in the world have looked at the same technology and produced forecasts that do not reconcile.

Daron Acemoglu — MIT economist, 2024 Nobel laureate — published a paper called "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI" and concluded that AI will raise total factor productivity by *no more than 0.71% over ten years*, and probably less than 0.55% once you account for the fact that the easy wins come first and the hard ones may never come. The OECD, modeling the same question for the G7 economies, projected annual labour-productivity gains of *0.4 to 1.3 percentage points* over a comparable horizon — in the highest-exposure economies, a transformation.

These are not amateurs disagreeing about a detail. They are two credible camps, using real data and defensible methods, arriving at conclusions that are not close. And here is the thing this book has been promising to do all along: it is not going to pretend the disagreement away. The honest-confidence rule that has run through every chapter becomes, in this one, the actual content. There are four big questions about AI and work where the evidence genuinely disagrees with itself, and the responsible move is to lay out both sides fairly, explain *why* the smart people diverge, and then show you how to act anyway.

This chapter resolves nothing. That is the point. Acting well when the experts genuinely disagree is a different skill from acting well when they agree, and it's the skill the rest of your career will require.

## Contested claim 1: the net macro impact

Take the Acemoglu–OECD gap seriously, because how you read it shapes how worried to be. [Contested]

**Acemoglu's case.** His method is a task-based model: aggregate gains roughly equal the fraction of tasks AI actually affects, times the average cost saving per task. He estimates only about 20% of U.S. tasks are meaningfully AI-exposed, and fewer than that are *profitably* automatable today. He then adjusts the forecast *downward*, reasoning that the early gains come from easy-to-learn tasks, while future gains would have to come from hard-to-learn tasks where AI is weaker — hence the move from ≤0.71% headline to <0.55% adjusted. He also argues AI need not reduce inequality and may widen the gap between capital and labour. The conservative macro anchor. [High as a clearly specified position; the forecast is contested by construction]

**The OECD's case.** Its model is dynamic. It combines micro-level estimates of performance gains with activity-level exposure and projected adoption paths, and it assumes stronger *reinstatement effects* — that AI creates enough genuinely new tasks and roles to offset what it displaces. High-exposure economies like the US and UK, specialized in AI-exposed knowledge-intensive services such as finance, see the largest gains. [High as a position]

**Why they diverge — and a units trap.** The headline numbers look farther apart than they really are, partly because they measure different things. Acemoglu quotes *total factor productivity, cumulative over ten years* — a single-digit-fraction figure by construction. The OECD quotes *labour productivity, per year* — a flow. You cannot simply subtract one from the other; they are not the same measure, and that mismatch inflates the apparent gulf. But normalize for it and a real disagreement remains, and it comes down to one assumption: **reinstatement effects.** Does AI create enough new work to offset the work it eliminates? Acemoglu is skeptical; the OECD is not. Nobody knows, because the new tasks haven't been created yet. This is the crux, and it is genuinely open.

History does not settle it cleanly either, which is part of why honest economists land in different places. Past general-purpose technologies — electricity, the computer — did eventually create enormous new categories of work, but with long and uneven lags, and Robert Solow's famous 1987 quip that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" is a reminder that the gains can hide for decades before showing up. An optimist reads that history as "the new tasks always came; be patient." A pessimist reads the same history as "the gains were real but smaller and slower than the hype, and this time the displaced tasks are cognitive, where reinstatement is less obvious." Both readings are defensible from the record. That is exactly why the macro question is *Contested* rather than merely uncertain: the evidence underdetermines the answer, and reasonable experts fill the gap with different priors.

## Contested claim 2: the durability timeline

This book has argued that judgment, causal reasoning, problem formulation, and institutional accountability are durable — the capacities AI can't perform. That claim has a soft underside, and intellectual honesty requires naming it. [Contested]

The durable-capacities thesis rests on a picture in which AI handles tasks while humans supply the surrounding judgment — the "coordination shell" of an occupation. But two lines of evidence suggest the shell may dissolve faster than that picture assumes. First, the "rising tides" research projects that AI will complete 80–95% of text-based tasks by 2029. Second, and more pointedly, the rise of *agentic* systems — AI that chains multiple steps, uses tools, and self-corrects across a whole workflow rather than answering one prompt at a time — means the unit of automation is shifting from the *task* to the *process*. The exposure indices this book and others rely on (AIOE, GPT-exposure) were built between 2018 and 2023 to measure *task-level* substitution. They were not designed to capture *end-to-end agentic workflow elimination*, and they may well underestimate it.

So the honest position is double-edged. The mechanisms for why judgment is durable (Chapters 4 through 7) are sound — AI genuinely does not perform causal identification, problem formulation, or accountability. But *how long* the surrounding human coordination work survives is contested, and the agentic frontier could compress the timeline faster than the tier hierarchy assumes. The capacities are durable. The job structures around them may be less so.

This distinction matters more than it first appears, and it changes what you should plant your flag on. If you bet your career on a *job structure* — "I am the person who coordinates these six tasks into a deliverable" — the agentic frontier is your direct competitor, because stitching tasks into an end-to-end workflow is exactly what agentic systems are built to do. If you bet on a *capacity* — "I am the person whose causal judgment is trustworthy when the stakes are real and the situation is novel" — you are betting on the thing that sits *outside* what even an agentic system performs. The contested timeline is a reason to anchor on capacities rather than roles, because capacities are robust to the timeline being short and roles are not. The honest uncertainty doesn't paralyze the decision; it sharpens it.

## Contested claim 3: the wage premium and what it means

PwC's 2025 "Global AI Jobs Barometer," built on roughly a billion job ads across six continents, reported that AI-skilled workers earned a 56% wage premium in 2024 — up from 25% the year before. Postings requiring AI skills grew 7.5% even as total postings fell 11.3%. The premium is real, and large. The question is *what it's a premium for.* [Contested]

The trouble is that "AI skills" in this data spans everything from basic Copilot use to machine-learning engineering. So the 56% conflates three different things: tool proficiency, domain expertise, and the *combination* of judgment-plus-domain-plus-AI-fluency that this book argues is durable. The premium for that durable combination has not been cleanly isolated from the premium for merely knowing how to use a chatbot.

Which means two readings are both live. If the premium is mostly a *scarcity rent* — paid because AI-fluent workers are temporarily rare — it will compress as AI literacy becomes table stakes, the way "knows how to use email" stopped commanding a premium. If it is mostly for the durable *combination*, it may be structural and persistent. The data can't yet tell these apart. [Medium that the premium exists; Contested on what it means] The practical upshot, if you're negotiating: the 56% is real, but don't bank on it as a permanent rent. It jumped from 25% to 56% in a single year, and a number that moves that fast can move again.

## Contested claim 4: leveler or hollower?

This is the one that confuses people most, and the resolution is that *both* sides are right at once. [Contested — present as "both true," not "pick one"]

**The leveler evidence is strong.** Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found a conversational AI assistant raised productivity about 14% on average — but about 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with little effect on the most experienced. It worked by disseminating the top performers' best practices down the experience curve. [High] Noy and Zhang ran a preregistered experiment with 444 college-educated professionals on writing tasks: time fell 40%, quality rose 18%, and *inequality between workers fell* because AI helped the lower-ability workers most. [High] On the tasks studied, AI compresses the gap between weak and strong workers. It is a leveler.

**The hollower evidence is equally strong** — and you've already seen it. Hosseini Maasoum and Lichtinger, and the Stanford ADP data (Chapters 2 and 11): AI *cuts the number of juniors hired*. [High for both halves]

These do not contradict. AI can be a leveler *within a job* — narrowing the gap between a novice and an expert on a given task — *and* a hollower *of the entry tier* — cutting how many people get hired into that job at all — at the same time. A reader in customer support lives the leveler effect directly; a reader hiring analysts lives the hollower effect. Same technology, opposite local experience. Which one dominates in aggregate differs by firm and sector and probably shifts over time, and the policy implications pull in opposite directions. The mistake is to pick one as *the* truth. Both are.

There's a darker possibility hiding inside the leveler finding, worth naming for honesty's sake. The Brynjolfsson study works by capturing the best performers' practices and disseminating them down the curve — which is wonderful for the novices it lifts, but it also means the firm has now *extracted and codified* what made its experts valuable. Once a top performer's judgment is encoded into a tool that brings a novice 80% of the way there, the scarcity premium on that expertise erodes. So "leveler" can mean "everyone gets better" and "the thing experts were paid for got commoditized" in the same breath. Whether that's a story of empowerment or of expertise being quietly mined depends entirely on which side of the curve you're standing on — which is, again, why the question resists a single verdict.

## The mechanism: why credible people disagree

Step back and notice that none of these disagreements is a case of one side being foolish. The macro fight comes down to an unobservable — reinstatement effects — that hasn't happened yet. The timeline fight comes down to measurement tools built for a previous generation of capability. The wage fight comes down to a definition ("AI skills") too broad to attribute. The leveler/hollower fight comes down to two real effects operating at two different levels. In every case the disagreement is *structural* — different defensible assumptions about genuinely unknown quantities — not a difference in competence. That is what real uncertainty looks like. It is not noise to be averaged away or a debate to be won. It is the actual state of knowledge.

## What it means for you

Here is the trap this chapter is designed to keep you out of: treating uncertainty as a reason for paralysis, or — worse — picking the forecast that matches your mood and acting as if it were settled. The doom reader picks Acemoglu's bleakest reading and freezes. The hype reader picks the OECD's brightest and over-commits. Both are pretending to a certainty the evidence doesn't support.

The way out is a decision rule borrowed from the science of *deep uncertainty* — the situation, formalized by RAND's Robust Decision Making program, where parties cannot agree on the models, the probabilities, or even how to value the outcomes. Under deep uncertainty, the goal is not a better forecast. It is a more *robust* decision: a choice that performs acceptably across many plausible futures, rather than optimally for one. You don't bet on which scenario is right. You bet on what pays off under all of them.

Run the four contested claims through that lens. Whether AI is a modest macro force (Acemoglu) or a transformative one (OECD); whether the durability timeline is long or short; whether the wage premium is rent or structure; whether AI levels or hollows — *in every one of those futures, durable judgment capacity pays off.* If AI is modest, your judgment was always the valuable part. If it's transformative, judgment is the scarce complement to abundant machine execution. If the timeline is long, you have time to compound it; if short, it's the only thing left standing. The robust bet doesn't require you to win the forecast. It requires you to invest in what's valuable regardless of who wins.

## The move

Make one no-regrets bet — and use the test that defines it.

A no-regrets action is one you'd be glad you took *no matter which scenario turns out to be true.* So for any career investment you're weighing under AI uncertainty, ask two questions: *Would I still want to have done this if AI's impact turns out smaller than feared? Would I still want to have done this if it turns out larger?* Keep only the bets that pass both.

Pick one this month. Deepening a durable capacity from Chapter 10 passes the test — judgment pays off in every scenario. Acquiring a narrow tool skill whose value depends entirely on AI staying exactly as capable as it is today probably fails it. The discipline is not to predict the future. It's to choose the moves that don't require you to.

## Bridge

You now have the whole picture: what's stratifying, what's durable, what erodes it, how to gate it, how to train it, how the ladder broke — and, honestly, where even the experts can't agree. The last thing left is to put it together into something you can actually hold: a single page, your situation, your plan. That's the conclusion.
