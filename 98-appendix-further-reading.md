# Appendix B — Further Reading by Chapter

> *An annotated bibliography. For each work, a one-paragraph description of what it contributes and where in the book it is most relevant.*

---

## Why this appendix exists

The book draws on many sources; the chapters cite the most-load-bearing ones but cannot devote space to extended reading recommendations. This appendix collects the recommendations by chapter and adds the works the chapters allude to but do not cite directly.

The appendix is opinionated. Each entry is a recommendation, not a survey. Readers who want more comprehensive bibliographies can find them in the cited works.

---

## Chapter 1 — The Turkey Problem

**Taleb, Nassim Nicholas. *The Black Swan: The Impact of the Highly Improbable*. 2nd ed. Random House, 2010.**
The popular source for the turkey example and the book-length statement of the underlying argument about rare events and statistical blindness. The 2nd edition adds the "Notes on Robustness and Fragility" appendix, which is the closest Taleb gets to a synthesized methodological statement.

**Hume, David. *A Treatise of Human Nature*. 1739–40.** *(Book I, Part III)*
The canonical statement of the problem of induction. The turkey is Hume's chicken in finance dress. Public domain.

**Brown, Stephen J., William N. Goetzmann, Roger G. Ibbotson, and Stephen A. Ross. "Survivorship Bias in Performance Studies." *Review of Financial Studies* 5, no. 4 (1992): 553–580.**
The formalization of survivorship bias for fund-performance studies. The paper is technical; the central finding (1–2 percentage-point overstatement annually from survivor selection) [verify] is the result the chapter relies on.

**Lewis, Michael. *The Big Short: Inside the Doomsday Machine*. W. W. Norton, 2010.**
The book-length narration of the 2008 mortgage-crisis case, with named characters running explicit mechanism analyses while their counterparties projected from history. The chapter uses this case directly.

---

## Chapter 2 — Known Unknowns

**Knight, Frank H. *Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit*. Houghton Mifflin, 1921.**
The pre-Rumsfeld articulation of the risk-vs-uncertainty distinction. Knight distinguishes *risk* (probability-quantifiable) from *uncertainty* (not), establishing the structural feature that makes the chapter's central distinction tractable. Public domain.

**Vaughan, Diane. *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA*. University of Chicago Press, 1996.**
The definitive analysis of how the O-ring vulnerability went from documented engineering concern to unconnected known. The *normalization of deviance* concept introduced here is essential for understanding many of the book's case studies.

**Valukas, Anton R. *Report of the Examiner — Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc.* United States Bankruptcy Court, Southern District of New York, 2010.**
The court-appointed Examiner's Report. Approximately 2,200 pages. The reference work for the Lehman case in Chapter 2 and the broader 2008 financial-crisis case material.

---

## Chapter 3 — The Mechanism Question (Pearl)

**Pearl, Judea, and Dana Mackenzie. *The Book of Why: The New Science of Cause and Effect*. Basic Books, 2018.**
The popular-audience statement of Pearl's causal framework. The ladder of causation, the do-calculus at the level of intuition, and the worked examples are all here. The starting point for non-technical readers.

**Pearl, Judea. *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference*. 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press, 2009.**
The technical reference. For readers who want the formal apparatus behind *The Book of Why*. Mathematically demanding; the standard reference for working in the framework.

**Hernán, Miguel A., and James M. Robins. *Causal Inference: What If*. Chapman & Hall/CRC, 2020.**
The bridge text between Pearl's framework and applied epidemiology / biostatistics. Strong on the practical question of when observational data can support causal conclusions.

**Snow, John. *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera*. 2nd ed. John Churchill, 1855.**
The primary source for the Broad Street pump investigation. Public domain. Short, readable, and a model of mechanism-based reasoning predating its formal mathematics by 140 years.

---

## Chapter 4 — The Constraint You Can't See (Goldratt)

**Goldratt, Eliyahu M., and Jeff Cox. *The Goal: A Process of Ongoing Improvement*. 4th ed. North River Press, 2014.**
The novelized introduction to Theory of Constraints. The chapter's case material is drawn from this and from Goldratt's later books. The 4th edition is the current reference.

**Goldratt, Eliyahu M. *The Theory of Constraints*. North River Press, 1990.**
The more formal articulation. Recommended after *The Goal* for readers who want the framework systematized.

**Mabin, Victoria J., and Steven J. Balderstone. "The performance of the theory of constraints methodology: Analysis and discussion of successful TOC applications." *International Journal of Operations and Production Management* 23, no. 6 (2003): 568–595.**
The primary published meta-analysis of TOC implementations. Evidence base for the chapter's claims about TOC effectiveness. Self-reported case data; methodological limits acknowledged.

**Forrester, Jay W. *Industrial Dynamics*. MIT Press, 1961.**
The pre-Goldratt foundation for system-level constraint analysis. System Dynamics as a discipline begins here. Less accessible than Goldratt but more rigorous on the underlying systems theory.

---

## Chapter 5 — The Assumption Underneath (Argyris)

**Argyris, Chris. *Overcoming Organizational Defenses: Facilitating Organizational Learning*. Allyn & Bacon, 1990.**
The accessible introduction to the defensive-reasoning framework. The ladder of inference is laid out here in usable form.

**Argyris, Chris, and Donald A. Schön. *Organizational Learning II: Theory, Method, and Practice*. Addison-Wesley, 1996.**
The mature statement of the espoused-theory / theory-in-use distinction. Builds on the earlier *Theory in Practice* (1974) and *Organizational Learning* (1978).

**Vaughan, Diane.** *(as cited Ch 2)* The Columbia case in Chapter 5 is documented in Vaughan's analysis as well as the *Columbia Accident Investigation Board Report* (2003, Volume 1).

**Edmondson, Amy C. *The Fearless Organization: Creating Psychological Safety in the Workplace for Learning, Innovation, and Growth*. Wiley, 2018.**
The successor to Argyris's work on the willingness-to-speak axis of organizational learning. Complementary; not a substitute.

---

## Chapter 6 — Practices That Surface

**Klein, Gary. "Performing a Project Premortem." *Harvard Business Review*, September 2007.**
The canonical short reference for the pre-mortem practice. Three pages; high information density.

**Wack, Pierre. "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead." *Harvard Business Review*, September–October 1985.**
The first of Wack's two *HBR* essays on Shell scenario planning. The companion piece — "Scenarios: Shooting the Rapids," *HBR*, November–December 1985 — extends the methodology. Both essential.

**van der Heijden, Kees. *Scenarios: The Art of Strategic Conversation*. 2nd ed. Wiley, 2005.**
The systematized treatment of scenario planning after Wack. The standard practitioner reference.

**Ohno, Taiichi. *Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production*. Productivity Press, 1988.**
The primary source for the Toyota production methodology and the five-whys practice. Short, dense, written by the production system's chief architect.

---

## Chapter 7 — Badly Priced Risk (Taleb)

**Taleb, Nassim Nicholas. *Fooled by Randomness: The Hidden Role of Chance in Life and in the Markets*. 2nd ed. Random House, 2005.**
The early statement of Taleb's framework. Includes the survivorship-bias-applied-to-the-analyst material that the chapter centers on. The earliest text in which the chapter's structural-irony question can be raised.

**Taleb, Nassim Nicholas. *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder*. Random House, 2012.**
The framework extended into the *response* side: not just identifying tail risk but designing for asymmetric exposure to it. The structural-response chapter of the book (Ch 10) draws on this.

**Mandelbrot, Benoit B., and Richard L. Hudson. *The (Mis)Behavior of Markets: A Fractal View of Financial Turbulence*. Basic Books, 2004.**
Taleb's explicit mentor on fat tails, in book form. More mathematically grounded than Taleb; the chapter cites for the underlying mathematics.

**Cassidy, John. "The Blow-Up Artist." *The New Yorker*, 2003 [verify exact date].**
The canonical profile of Taleb during the Empirica period. Source material for the chapter's case construction.

---

## Chapter 8 — When the Whole Model Breaks (Kuhn)

**Kuhn, Thomas S. *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. 4th ed. University of Chicago Press, 2012.**
The 50th-anniversary edition with introductory essay by Ian Hacking. The text remains as it appeared in the 2nd edition (1970, with the influential postscript). Hacking's introduction is helpful for situating the book's reception history.

**Fleck, Ludwik. *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact*. University of Chicago Press, 1979 (English; original German 1935).**
The pre-Kuhn predecessor. Kuhn cited Fleck explicitly. Less famous, more empirically grounded. Recommended for readers who want the under-acknowledged source.

**Lakatos, Imre. *The Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes: Philosophical Papers Volume 1*. Cambridge University Press, 1978.**
The post-Kuhn refinement. The hard-core / protective-belt distinction is in here. More granular than Kuhn's binary normal/revolution.

**Womack, James P., Daniel T. Jones, and Daniel Roos. *The Machine That Changed the World*. Free Press, 1990.**
The book-length analysis of the Detroit / Toyota production-system shift. The chapter's case material on the auto-industry paradigm change.

---

## Chapter 9 — The Collective Blind Spot (Senge)

**Senge, Peter M. *The Fifth Discipline: The Art and Practice of the Learning Organization*. Revised and updated ed. Doubleday, 2006.**
The systematized statement of the five disciplines. The 2006 revision is the current reference.

**Senge, Peter M., Charlotte Roberts, Richard B. Ross, Bryan J. Smith, and Art Kleiner. *The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook*. Doubleday, 1994.**
The practitioner companion. Operationalizes the framework with worked exercises, case studies, and practical materials. Recommended for readers planning to apply the framework rather than only read about it.

**Meadows, Donella H. *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*. Chelsea Green, 2008 (posthumous).**
Edited from Meadows's manuscripts. The most accessible introduction to systems-thinking concepts. Pairs well with Senge.

**Forrester, Jay W.** *(as cited Ch 4)* The MIT-tradition foundation for systems thinking. Recommended for readers who want the rigor underneath Senge's pragmatic framing.

---

## Chapter 10 — Seeing the System

**Perrow, Charles. *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*. Updated ed. Princeton University Press, 1999.**
The book-length argument that catastrophic accidents are *structurally* produced in complex tightly-coupled systems — not occasional individual failures. The chapter's resilience framing draws on this.

**Weick, Karl E., and Kathleen M. Sutcliffe. *Managing the Unexpected: Sustained Performance in a Complex World*. 3rd ed. Jossey-Bass, 2015.**
The high-reliability-organizations literature in book form. *Mindfulness* as the operational property that distinguishes HROs from peer organizations.

**Hollnagel, Erik, David D. Woods, and Nancy Leveson, eds. *Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts*. Ashgate, 2006.**
The foundational text of resilience engineering. Technical; for readers who want the formal framework underneath the chapter's structural-response discussion.

**Taleb, Nassim Nicholas. *Antifragile*.** *(as cited Ch 7)* The structural-response argument in Taleb's voice.

## Cross-chapter — synthesis sources

These are works the book draws on across multiple chapters but does not assign to any one.

**Kahneman, Daniel. *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.**
The reference work on individual cognitive bias. Used in Chs 2, 5, 7. Read after the other chapters; it provides the individual-cognition substrate that the book's organizational and systems-level analyses sit on top of.

**Levitin, Daniel J., et al.** *(the field-survey literature on statistical reasoning failures — see Calling Bullshit by Bergstrom & West, 2020; Damned Lies and Statistics by Best, 2001; How to Lie with Statistics by Huff, 1954)*. The chapter's general engagement with the family of inductive-fallacy literature.

**Gladwell, Malcolm. *Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking*. Little, Brown, 2005.**
Useful as a counter-point on intuition. The book's overall position is that intuition is real but bounded by the practitioner's prior coverage; Gladwell makes the case for the intuition side.

**Tetlock, Philip E., and Dan Gardner. *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction*. Crown, 2015.**
The accessible statement of Tetlock's empirical work on what makes some forecasters better than others. The chapter draws on the underlying expert-judgment research.
