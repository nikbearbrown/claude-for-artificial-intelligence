# Problem Formulation

*Deciding which problem is worth solving — the work that cannot be prompted.*

## "We need a dashboard"

A product manager forwards a request from the head of sales: "We need a dashboard showing pipeline by region, updated daily." It is a clear, well-formed ask. An analyst could prompt an AI to scaffold the whole thing in an afternoon — schema, queries, a clean front end. Every word of the request is actionable.

It is also the wrong problem.

A week of conversations reveals what the request was standing in for. Sales already *has* a pipeline view. Nobody trusts the numbers in it, because two teams enter deal stages by different conventions and the totals never reconcile with what reps say in forecast calls. The real problem is not visualization. It is that the organization has no shared definition of what counts as a "committed" deal, and a beautiful daily dashboard built on that disagreement would make the distrust *worse* — now the bad numbers would be authoritative, automated, and on everyone's screen by 9 a.m. The problem worth solving was "we don't have a trusted definition of pipeline." No prompt produced that reframing, because the person who wrote the brief didn't know that was the problem either. It had to be *found*.

That finding — the move from the problem as handed to you to the problem actually worth solving — is the subject of this chapter. It is the capacity that lives furthest upstream of any prompt, and I want to be honest from the first paragraph about how we know what we know about it.

## The evidence — and an honest accounting of its strength

I'll state the confidence before the claim, because this chapter is where the book's honesty rule earns its keep. **The central claim here rests on theory and analogy, not on a controlled experiment [Medium].** There is no RCT showing that AI degrades human problem-formulation skill, and no clean benchmark showing AI *can't* formulate problems. What there is, is a deep and convergent tradition in how professionals actually work — and a narrow, defensible version of the claim that the theory genuinely supports. I'm going to give you the theory and then narrow the claim to exactly what it can carry.

The tradition starts with Donald Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) [High as a theory]. Schön's central observation was that competent professional work is not mainly problem-*solving*. It is **problem-*setting*** — "the process by which we define the decision to be made, the ends to be achieved, the means which may be chosen." Real problems, he wrote, do not present themselves as well-formed givens. They arrive in what he called the "swampy lowland" — messy, indeterminate, value-laden — and the practitioner's first and hardest act is to *name* what to attend to and *frame* the situation so that it becomes workable at all. Technical rationality — the machinery of solving — operates only after the swamp has been drained into a defined problem. It cannot do the draining.

Kees Dorst, in *Frame Innovation* (2015), gives this a modern operational shape — problem and solution co-evolving, framing as the creative act [Medium-High as theory]. A **frame**, in his terms, is "a coherent set of statements about the problem that are useful to think with." **Frame creation** is the leap to a new lens that redefines what the problem even is. And the companion idea — **problem–solution co-evolution** (Dorst & Cross, 2001) — is the one that matters most for AI: in real design work, your understanding of the problem and your candidate solutions develop *together*, each reshaping the other. You half-build a solution, discover it answers the wrong question, and that discovery reframes the problem. A generative system handed a fixed prompt short-circuits exactly this loop. It optimizes a solution to the *given* framing with great fluency, and it never re-frames, because re-framing was not in the ask.

Then there is the one piece of older empirical work worth leaning on: Getzels and Csikszentmihalyi's longitudinal study of art students, *The Creative Vision* (1976) [Medium]. They found that how much an artist *explored and reformulated the problem* before committing to it predicted later creative achievement better than technical skill did. Problem-finding, not problem-solving, distinguished the work that mattered. It is old, and it is art rather than knowledge work, but it is a real data point that formulation is where the value concentrates.

And the pedagogical spine — which recurs in the deskilling and training chapters — is cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman, 1989) [High as pedagogy]. Its six methods culminate in **exploration**: pushing the learner into framing their own problems and setting their own goals, not just executing given ones. This is the *last* thing an apprentice acquires and the thing that marks the transition to autonomous expertise.

There is a nice irony in the deepest root of this, worth naming because it complicates any triumphalist reading. Herbert Simon — who, with Allen Newell, founded much of the field that became AI — was also the theorist of **ill-structured problems** (Simon, 1973). Simon's point was that most consequential real-world problems are not "well-structured": they lack a clear initial state, a defined goal, and a fixed set of operators for getting from one to the other. They are ill-structured precisely because *deciding what the problem is* is part of the problem. A chess position is well-structured; "should this company enter that market" is not, and no amount of computational power on the second resolves the prior question of which market, which definition of "enter," and which of a dozen rival goals you are actually optimizing. The man who helped build artificial intelligence was clear that the hard part lives upstream of the solving — and that the upstream part is structuring the problem, which is the work this chapter is about.

## The mechanism — and the narrow claim it licenses

Here is why a prompt cannot resolve an underspecified brief, traced carefully.

A generative model is a *solution engine*. Give it a well-formed problem and it is extraordinary; that is most of what this book concedes the tools are good at. But a solution engine *presupposes a formulated problem*. The formulation — the decision about which problem, out of the many latent in a messy situation, is worth solving — has to come from somewhere upstream. The dashboard request *was* a formulation; it was just a poor one, because the person who wrote it was solving for "make the discomfort go away" without diagnosing the discomfort. An AI handed that brief will build the dashboard beautifully. It has no purchase on the question of whether the dashboard is the right object at all.

Now I have to be fair to the counter-argument, because it is real and the honesty rule demands it. Agentic systems increasingly *do* ask clarifying questions. Prompt one with a vague brief and it will often propose reframings, surface ambiguities, suggest that maybe the problem is trust rather than visualization. This is genuine, and it means the strong claim — "AI can't reframe problems" — is false. So here is the narrow claim, which is the one the theory actually supports and the one to hold:

**AI cannot originate, from unstated institutional context and lived values, the judgment about which problem is worth solving** [Medium — theory and analogy, not RCT]. It can reframe *within the information you give it.* It cannot supply the things that aren't in the prompt and aren't in its training data: that *these two sales teams* have a history, that the VP's real fear is the board meeting in three weeks, that a "trust" framing will land politically where a "tooling" framing will not, that this problem matters more than the other six on your list because of where the organization is *this quarter*. Those are not information gaps a clarifying question fills. They are tacit, contextual, and value-laden — and the choice among problems they imply is the irreducible human residue. The reframe a clever prompt produces is a mechanical reframe within given data. The *value judgment about which problem is worth your scarce attention* is the durable part. Keep the claim there.

And carry the durability honestly: this is durable on the current frontier, and agentic reframing is improving. The safe bet is not "formulation is permanently human." It is "the contextual origination and the value judgment are yours for now."

## What it means for you

The implication reorders where you spend effort. If the tools are solution engines, then the marginal value of being a faster solver is collapsing — that is the labor stratification this whole book describes. The value migrates upstream, to the person who decides *which problem enters the solving machine.* In every field the structure repeats. The client says "we want to sue"; the formulated problem might be "preserve the relationship while protecting the IP" — a different problem with entirely different solutions. The stakeholder says "make it faster"; the real bottleneck is onboarding, not runtime. The patient presents a complaint; the clinical problem is the diagnosis behind the complaint. The researcher must choose the *question* — the one that is at once fundable, answerable, and worth answering, the FINER-style judgment no prompt resolves because it depends on context the asker hasn't stated. In each case the handed-down brief is *a* problem, plausibly the wrong one, and the professional's distinctive contribution is the reframe.

Here is the co-evolution idea made concrete, because it's the part that's easy to nod at and hard to feel. Take the dashboard again, and watch two framings produce two genuinely different worlds. *Framing A — "build a dashboard":* the solution space fills with chart types, refresh cadences, data pipelines, access controls. Every option is about display. *Framing B — "establish a trusted definition of pipeline":* the solution space fills with something else entirely — a cross-team working session to agree on what "committed" means, a one-page data dictionary, a weekly reconciliation ritual, maybe *no dashboard at all* until the definitions settle. The two solution spaces barely overlap. And notice the direction of the arrow: you often can't see which framing is right until you've sketched a solution in each and watched one of them answer a question nobody was actually asking. That is what Dorst means by co-evolution, and it is exactly the loop a fixed prompt forecloses — the AI commits to Framing A's solution space the instant you type "build a dashboard," and never discovers that the problem was somewhere else.

There is also an atrophy hook, and it's worth naming even though the evidence for it is the thinnest in the book. If the AI reliably hands you both the framing and the solution, you stop practicing the exploration phase — the autonomous problem-setting that cognitive apprenticeship says is the last and most fragile thing experts acquire. The plausible failure mode (asserted, not demonstrated [Medium]) is *homogenization*: heavy AI use nudging everyone toward whatever framing the model's training distribution treats as the default problem. Whether that actually happens at the population level is an open empirical question no one has answered. I flag it as a risk, not a finding.

This bears especially on early-career professionals, and it connects to the seniority squeeze this book describes elsewhere. The traditional way you learned to formulate problems was by being handed badly-formed ones and *struggling* with them — the junior analyst who is told "look into the Northeast numbers" and has to discover, painfully, that the real question is about a single mislabeled product line. That struggle was the exploration phase, and it was a costless byproduct of junior work. When the AI absorbs the struggle — when it cheerfully proposes a framing and a solution before the junior has wrestled with the mess — the byproduct disappears. The work still gets done; the *learning* that the work used to manufacture does not. The risk is not that any single professional gets dumber. It is that the apprenticeship which turned juniors into people with formulation judgment quietly stops running, and we discover in five years that we have a generation fluent at prompting solution engines and untrained at choosing which problem to point them at. That is a hypothesis [Low-Medium], not a measured outcome — but it is the hypothesis that should worry a manager most.

There is a second-order consequence worth stating plainly, because it changes how you should read the AI's confidence. If you cannot formulate the problem yourself, you also cannot tell whether the AI's fluent solution is a solution to the *right* problem — you have lost the only vantage point from which the error is visible. The dashboard would have looked like a success. It compiled, it shipped, it was even used. The failure — that it was the wrong object entirely — is legible only to someone who held the reframe in their head. So the formulation skill is not just an input to good work; it is the *audit faculty* for AI-produced work, the same way independent causal reasoning was the audit faculty in the previous chapter. Lose it and you don't just formulate worse — you supervise blind.

## The move: reject the prompt before you run it

Take a problem someone has handed you — a brief, a ticket, a request, an ask in a meeting. Before you prompt anything, before you build anything, write four short things. It takes fifteen minutes and it is Schön's problem-setting turned into a repeatable habit.

**One: write the problem exactly as given.** One sentence. "Build a daily pipeline dashboard by region." Getting it down verbatim makes its assumptions visible.

**Two: write two alternative framings of the same situation.** Force yourself past the first. "The problem is that no one trusts the pipeline numbers." "The problem is that two teams define deal stages differently." Each framing implies a different solution — that's the test that they're genuinely different framings and not restatements.

**Three: name which framing actually matters, and why — in terms of context the prompt didn't contain.** This is the load-bearing step, because it's the one the AI cannot do for you. Why does the trust framing matter more than the visualization framing *here, now, for these people*? If your answer references something not in the original brief — a relationship, a deadline, a political reality, a value — you've done the irreducibly human work.

**Four: *now* prompt the AI, against the framing you chose.** Not the one you were handed. The tool is excellent at solving; let it solve the right problem.

The discipline is in the order. The handed-down brief is seductive precisely because it's well-formed and ready to execute — you can start *being productive* immediately. Rejecting it for fifteen minutes feels like friction. It is the friction that separates solving the wrong problem fast from solving the right problem at all.

Two cautions, because this move is easy to do badly. First, "reject the prompt" is not an invitation to be contrarian — reframing every request as secretly being about something deeper is its own failure mode, and it makes you exhausting to work with. The discipline is not "the stated problem is always wrong." It is "the stated problem is *a hypothesis* about what's worth solving, and I owe it fifteen minutes of scrutiny before I commit my effort and the AI's." Sometimes the dashboard really is the right object, and you'll know because you checked. Second, the move only works if step three references something *outside the brief*. If your "which framing matters" answer can be derived from the words you were handed, you haven't reframed — you've reworded. The whole point is to import the unstated context, the institutional knowledge, the value judgment that no prompt contained. That import is the work the machine cannot do, and it is the only part of this exercise that AI-proofs you. The rest — generating alternative framings, even evaluating them on stated criteria — a capable model can increasingly help with. The irreducible step is the one where you supply what isn't in the room.

## Bridge

Choosing which problem is worth solving often means weighing things that have cost something — relationships, reputations, values under pressure. That judgment doesn't live in any one head; it lives in rooms full of people who are accountable for the call. The next chapter is about what survives there: trust, institutional context, and the accountability AI can describe but cannot bear.
