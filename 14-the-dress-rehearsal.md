# Chapter 14 — The Dress Rehearsal

An orchestra does not walk from the last day of sectional practice straight onto the stage. There is a dress rehearsal: the whole piece played through, once, in the hall, with someone in the empty seats whose only job is to hear the wrong note before the audience does. The point of the rehearsal is not to confirm that the orchestra is good. The musicians already believe they are good. The point is to find out whether the performance survives being *heard* — by someone who was not in the room when the parts were learned, who carries no memory of how hard the second movement was to drill, who will simply register what is actually there.

Your Supervisory Analysis is finished, and you believe it is sound. That belief was built in private. It has never been heard. A supervisory analysis that has not been challenged is not yet finished — not because it is wrong, but because you do not yet know whether the accountability in it survives scrutiny from someone who was not in the room. The dress rehearsal is where you find out, against peers, before you find out against an adversary that does not get tired.

And the rehearsal has one instrument sharper than all the others: before you submit to the auditor, you will write down exactly what you think the auditor will find. Anticipating your own refutation is itself a supervisory act — arguably the purest one in the course.

## Peer Critique as a Structured Instrument

The first misconception to kill is that feedback is niceness. In most settings, "peer feedback" means a colleague reading your work and reporting how it made them feel — encouraging, a little confused here, nice job overall. That is vibes. It is not critique, and it will not survive contact with the auditor.

In the dress rehearsal, peer critique is a **structured instrument**: the five-capacity framework applied as a checklist of evidence-bearing findings. The rule that makes it an instrument is simple and absolute — **a finding without located evidence is not a finding.** "Your integration feels weak" is not a finding. "Section 5 sequences rather than integrates, because the Re-engagement Log contains no trigger connecting the formulation in §2 to the interpretation in §4, even though §4's legitimacy account clearly depends on §2's scope decision" — *that* is a finding. It names the capacity, points to the location, and states the evidence. The author can act on it.

This is not a stylistic preference. The empirical literature on peer assessment is consistent: structured, rubric-anchored, evidence-required critique outperforms open-ended feedback, and established systems such as Calibrated Peer Review are built on exactly that principle [Medium-High]. The five-capacity framework is your rubric. Every finding cites the capacity, the location, and the evidence — or it does not count.

A second misconception, quieter and more damaging: *more findings make a better critique.* They do not. A few located, evidenced findings are worth more than a long vague list, because each one can be acted on and each one can be defended or accepted on its merits. The critic's job is not volume. It is to find the seams.

There is a discipline to *where* you look, too. The framework tells you not just to find flaws but to find them at the five named load points, and — taking the lesson of Simon's seams from the last chapter — to weight your attention toward the couplings between sections rather than the interior of any one. A critic who reports that §1's plausibility audit is thorough and §3's orchestration is well-documented has confirmed what is cheap to confirm. A critic who notices that §1 flagged a data source as needing verification and §3 then used that source without recording the check has found a seam — a cross-section coupling that failed. The most valuable peer findings, almost always, are the ones that name a relationship between two sections that each looked fine alone. Train your eye there. That is also, not coincidentally, exactly where the AI auditor will aim in Chapter 15, which is the second reason the rehearsal is worth doing: it teaches you to read your own work the way the adversary will.

## Three Legitimate Responses to a Critique

When a finding lands on your desk, the reflexive student move is to accept it. Good students, especially, over-index on this: critique arrives, the student revises, gratitude all around. This instinct is a supervisory failure waiting to happen, because **not every finding is correct.** A critique can misread the evidence. A critique can object to wording that is actually precise. And a supervisor who accepts a misreading has just let someone else's error launder itself into their signed work.

There are three legitimate responses, and choosing correctly among them is the skill:

1. **Revise.** The finding is correct — the evidence is located, the reading is right, the work is genuinely weaker than it should be. You change the analysis and you log *why*. A revision without recorded reasoning is just churn.

2. **Defend.** The finding misread the evidence. The critic pointed at §3 and called a handoff undocumented, but the trust decision is in fact stated two sentences later. You hold your ground — *with reasoning.* You record the finding, you state precisely where the critic's reading went wrong, and you leave the original wording. **Defending against a misreading is as much a supervisory act as revising in response to a correct one.** This is the path students most need to practice and most resist, and it is the same skill Chapter 15 will demand against the AI auditor: evaluating a finding as a genuine failure or a false positive. The rehearsal trains it first against peers.

3. **Acknowledge.** The finding is partly right — it points to a real limitation you cannot fully resolve within scope, or a tension you must live with. You neither revise it away nor defend it as a non-issue. You name it, in the analysis, as a known limit. Acknowledgment is honest where revision would be false and defense would be evasive.

Every response — revise, defend, acknowledge — goes into the **revision log**: the finding, your response category, and the reasoning. The log is the artifact. It demonstrates that critique was processed by judgment rather than absorbed by reflex.

## Predicting the Auditor's Findings

Here is the rehearsal's sharpest move, and the chapter's reason for existing where it does in the sequence. Before you submit your analysis to the adversarial auditor in Chapter 15, you will write down the **three most likely findings the auditor will produce.** This prediction is recorded and graded against the auditor's actual findings in Week 15.

### AI Wayback Machine — Karl Popper, *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963)

> *Figure 14.1 — Lay down the refutation criteria beforehand.*
>
> In 1963, Karl Popper collected the essays and lectures of *Conjectures and Refutations: The Growth of Scientific Knowledge.* Its spine is the principle of **falsifiability**: a theory is scientific only if it *forbids* something — only if it makes a risky prediction that could turn out wrong. A theory compatible with every possible observation explains nothing.
>
> The move this chapter borrows is almost a literal quotation. Popper argues that "criteria of refutation have to be laid down beforehand: it must be agreed which observable situations, if actually observed, mean that the theory is refuted." State, in advance, what would prove you wrong. That is precisely the prediction exercise: state, in advance, what the auditor will find. It is one of the tightest matches between an ancestor and a practice in this entire book [High].
>
> And carry forward the nuance, because it sets up the end of the course. For Popper, surviving refutation is **corroboration, not proof.** A theory that withstands sincere attempts to refute it has earned standing — it is not-yet-refuted — but it is never *verified*, never proven true. Corroboration is provisional. This matters enormously in Chapter 15: a clean audit does not prove your analysis is right. It proves only that this auditor, this time, did not refute it. The graduation condition of the course is not a clean audit. Hold that thought; it is the hinge between this chapter and the next.

Two things make the prediction more than a guess. First, **calibration is trainable.** The metacognitive research on prediction-then-feedback — predict your performance, then check the prediction against reality — generally finds that the practice improves calibration, and that the *worst-calibrated* learners tend to improve the most [Medium-High; the direction is well supported, though some classroom studies report persistent miscalibration despite feedback, and effect sizes vary by study, so no single number is quoted here]. So if your prediction is wrong, that is not a failure of the exercise; it is the exercise working. Being wrong in a *specific* way is informative in a way that being right vaguely is not.

Second — and this is the Popperian demand applied to your own work — **the prediction must be specific and falsifiable.** "The auditor will find weak integration" is unfalsifiable mush; almost any finding could be read as confirming it. A real prediction reads: *"The auditor will flag that §5 sequences rather than integrates, because there is no documented re-engagement trigger linking my formulation choice in §2 to the legitimacy account in §4."* That prediction forbids something. It can be scored — matched, partially matched, or missed — and whichever happens, you learn something located. A vague prediction teaches nothing because nothing could contradict it.

The pre-mortem — Gary Klein's technique of imagining, before you act, that the project has already failed and asking why — is the close cousin here: anticipate the failure before it happens so you can see it while there is still time to act [a one-line companion to Popper's logic].

## A Validity Caveat, Taught Honestly

There is a real problem with grading a prediction against an AI auditor, and the course names it rather than hiding it. The auditor is an LLM, and LLM behavior can **drift between model versions.** Your prediction could miss not because you misjudged your own work but because the auditor changed between cohorts. The honest response — and the one the rubric is built on — is to grade the *specificity and reasoning of your predictions,* not the raw hit-rate against a possibly-drifting target. A precisely-reasoned prediction that the auditor happens not to confirm is still excellent supervisory work. A lucky vague hit is not. *(Open rubric-design question for the instructor: weight specificity and reasoning over raw hit-rate, precisely to absorb auditor model drift — see the Risk Register.)* There is, as of 2025–2026, no established literature on calibrating human predictions against an *LLM* auditor specifically; the chapter extrapolates from the robust human-grader calibration research, and you should know it is an extrapolation [Medium].

## What Peer Challenge Produces That Solo Review Cannot

You self-audited your analysis in Chapter 13 against the three failure modes. Why is that not enough? Because you cannot fully audit the thing you cannot see, and the work you produced is the work whose blind spots you share. A peer brings a *different* set of blind spots — and where their sightlines cover your blind spots, findings appear that solo review structurally could not produce. This is the same principle that governed cross-auditing in Chapter 9 (tools with different failure modes catch each other's errors) and the same principle that will define the *limits* of the AI auditor in Chapter 15 (an auditor that shares your blind spots cannot find them). The dress rehearsal is cross-auditing with humans. It catches what you could not catch alone — and it teaches you, by contrast, exactly what kind of finding *no* outside reviewer, human or machine, will be able to reach. That last category is what you will name in your Gap Account.

## A Worked Contested Finding

A peer critiques a clinical analyst's Supervisory Analysis. Finding: *"§4 lacks a moral-legitimacy account; you diagnosed that the AI's recommendation was pragmatically legitimate but never supplied moral legitimacy — failure mode three."*

The analyst examines the evidence and finds the critique half-right. §4 *does* supply moral legitimacy — but only for the patient, not for the clinic's other patients whose access is affected by the recommended protocol change. So:

- On the patient-level legitimacy: **defend.** The account is there, in the third paragraph of §4, with the value commitment named. The critic missed it. Logged, with the location cited, original wording held.
- On the population-level legitimacy: **acknowledge** — escalating to **revise.** The critic surfaced a real gap the analyst had not seen. The analyst adds a population-access legitimacy account, logs the change and its reasoning, and notes that this re-opened §2 (the problem had been framed at the individual-patient level and needed to widen) — a new Re-engagement Log entry, produced by the rehearsal itself.

One finding, two response paths, both reasoned, both logged. That is the dress rehearsal working as designed.

## Where This Leaves You

Your analysis has been heard. It survived peers — some findings revised, some defended, some acknowledged, all logged. And a specific, falsifiable, three-part prediction of the auditor's findings is on the record, where it cannot be quietly revised after the fact. You have laid down, beforehand, the criteria by which you could be refuted.

## Exercises

**Exercise 14.1 — Structured peer critique (Evaluate).** Critique a peer's Supervisory Analysis using the five-capacity framework as the rubric. Every finding must name the capacity, locate the evidence, and state the reading. Aim for depth, not volume: a small set of located, evidenced findings. *(Deliverable: written critique. Part of the **100-point Peer Critique and Revised Analysis** assessment.)*

**Exercise 14.2 — Revised analysis with revision log (Create).** Revise your own analysis in response to the critiques you received. For each finding, record your response — revise, defend, or acknowledge — with the reasoning. At least one finding should be *defended* against a misreading, with the evidence the critic missed cited precisely. *(Deliverable: revised analysis plus the complete revision log. Part of the 100-point assessment.)*

**Exercise 14.3 — Predict the auditor (Evaluate).** Write your three most likely audit findings. Each must be specific and falsifiable — naming the capacity, the section, and the evidence the auditor will cite. "The auditor will find X in §N because Y." These predictions are recorded now and graded in Week 15 against the auditor's actual findings, with grading weighted toward specificity and reasoning rather than raw hit-rate. *(Deliverable: three predicted findings on the prediction scorecard, which carries forward to Chapter 15. Surface the Assessment Spine: for at least one prediction, state why anticipating this particular weakness required judgment about your own work that you could not have delegated.)*

## Bridge to Chapter 15

The analysis has survived collegial scrutiny, and a prediction is on record. Now it faces an adversary of a different kind — Claude, instructed to function as a Plausibility Auditor, hunting your undocumented handoffs, your defaulted formulations, your legitimacy accounts that diagnose a gap without filling it, your integration sections that sequence rather than integrate. It does not get tired. It does not extend the benefit of the doubt a peer extends. And — carrying Popper forward — even if it finds nothing, that is corroboration, not proof. The graduation condition is not a clean audit. It is something the auditor, by its nature, cannot produce.
