# Supervisory Judgment and the Calibration Trap

*The skill of catching the fluent, confident, wrong answer — and the trap that erodes it.*

## A brief that reads beautifully and cites a case that does not exist

A litigation associate hands a partner a motion to dismiss. It is clean. The structure is textbook: a crisp statement of the standard, four authorities marshaled in a tidy line, each summarized in a sentence that lands exactly where the argument needs it to land. The associate drafted it in twenty minutes with an AI assistant and read it twice. It reads, as the associate later put it, "like something that had already won."

One of the four cases does not exist. Not "was decided differently" — does not exist. The reporter citation points to a volume and page that hold an unrelated opinion; the holding the brief attributes to it was assembled by a language model that had learned what a citation of that shape *sounds like* and produced one. This is not a hypothetical: courts in several jurisdictions have sanctioned lawyers for filing AI-fabricated authorities since 2023, and the pattern is always the same. The fabrication is invisible *because the prose is good.* The associate didn't skip the check out of laziness. They skipped it because nothing in the document signaled that a check was needed. It felt finished.

That feeling — the sense that a fluent output is a correct one — is the subject of this chapter. It is also, as we'll see, the most dangerous instinct you bring to AI-assisted work, and the one the book's whole defense depends on your overriding.

## The evidence: you are a worse judge of your own work when AI helps

The capacity that matters here is **supervisory judgment** — the ability to catch the confident, polished, wrong answer before it becomes your answer. In a world where AI produces first drafts of nearly everything, this is the human layer that the thesis of this book says survives: the machine generates, you supervise. It is, in principle, durable. Generative AI is a next-token predictor; it has no internal sense of whether what it just said is true, and no faculty for auditing itself against reality. Someone has to. That someone is you.

Here is the problem, stated as plainly as the evidence allows: **the act of working with AI appears to degrade the very self-monitoring that supervision requires — and to degrade it most in the people who feel most equipped to supervise.**

The central study is Fernandes and colleagues, *AI Makes You Smarter, But None the Wiser* (2024/2025, arXiv:2409.16708) [Medium-High]. It is one paper with two experiments. In the first (N = 246), participants solved LSAT-style logical-reasoning problems with AI assistance. Their performance rose — roughly three points above a comparison population. But their *estimate of their own performance* rose more: they overshot their actual scores by about four points. They got better and felt better than "better" warranted. The second experiment (N = 452 — the figure this book cites) reproduced the disconnect.

Then the finding that should stop you: **higher self-reported AI literacy correlated with *worse* self-assessment accuracy.** The people who described themselves as more knowledgeable about AI were more confident and *less* precise about how well they had actually done. The authors also report, using a computational model, that the familiar Dunning-Kruger pattern — weak performers wildly overconfident, strong performers slightly underconfident — *flattened* under AI use. Everyone drifted toward a roughly uniform overconfidence.

Hold the confidence label firmly here. This is **[Medium-High]**, not High, and the reasons matter. It is a single paper, two studies, perhaps seven hundred people total, on lab reasoning items rather than real professional work, and the "AI literacy" result is new and under-replicated. It is the single most load-bearing *contestable* claim in this chapter, and you should treat it as a strong signal rather than a settled law. But it points in a direction that decades of older work also point.

That older work is solid [High]. Processing fluency — the subjective ease with which something is read or understood — reliably inflates judgments of truth, familiarity, and confidence (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009). Fluent prose *feels* true; that is a well-established property of human cognition, not a quirk of AI. And automation bias — the tendency to accept what an automated system tells us, producing both errors of omission (missing what the system missed) and commission (doing the wrong thing the system suggested) — is documented in experts, not only novices, going back to the aviation studies of the 1990s (Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, 1999) [High].

There is one more piece, and it cuts both ways. Explanations sometimes help. Von Zahn and colleagues (*Information Systems Research*, 2025) ran incentivized experiments in which domain experts chose, task by task, whether to defer to an AI [Medium]. Good explanations *improved* their calibration — but specifically when the explanation revealed that the AI's reasoning genuinely diverged from the human's, which punctured overconfidence. The mirror image of that finding — that a fluent, plausible explanation for a *wrong* answer can deepen overconfidence — is mechanistically consistent with everything above, but it is the inverse framing of what that paper directly demonstrated. So treat "good explanations help, fluent-but-wrong ones hurt" as **mechanism-supported [Medium]**, not as a clean experimental result.

It is worth pausing on what is genuinely new here, because the comfortable prior is the one being overturned. For thirty years the story about automation bias was a story about *novices* — undertrained operators over-trusting systems they didn't understand, with the implied remedy being training and expertise. The 2024–2025 work (Fernandes, von Zahn) studies something else: *experts collaborating with generative AI*, and it finds the unsettling twist that expertise and AI literacy do not reliably protect calibration and may worsen it. If that holds, it inverts the reassurance every professional has been telling themselves — "I'll be fine, I know what I'm doing." Knowing what you're doing is exactly the condition under which the fluent output feels most at home, and home is where you stop checking the locks.

## The mechanism: why the polish disarms the check

Trace it carefully, because the mechanism is what makes the defense designable.

A language model produces text by predicting likely continuations. Its output is optimized to be *probable* — to read like the kind of thing that follows from your prompt — and probable text is, by construction, fluent text. Fluency is the model's native register. It does not produce halting, hedged, visibly-uncertain prose when it is wrong and confident prose when it is right; it produces confident prose either way, because confidence is a feature of the surface, not a readout of any internal truth-check. The model has no internal truth-check.

Now bring in the human side. Your mind uses ease-of-processing as a cue for correctness — a heuristic that works well in a world where things that are easy to read were usually written by someone who understood them. AI breaks that world. It manufactures the *signal* of competence (smooth, structured, confident text) decoupled from the *substance* of competence. So the fluent-but-wrong output hits you with a strong "this is fine" cue and a missing "look closer" cue. Your System 2 — the slow, effortful, checking part of cognition — never gets summoned, because nothing felt hard.

This is why the older disfluency research matters (Alter et al., 2007) [High]: when a problem is made *harder* to process — a degraded font, a deliberate pause — people rely less on their wrong first intuition and engage analytic checking. Difficulty triggers the audit. Fluency suppresses it. AI output is maximally fluent, which means it is maximally good at suppressing the audit you most need.

Layer the Fernandes finding on top and you get the trap in full. It is not merely that AI output is hard to audit. It is that *using* AI appears to inflate your confidence in your own judgment — most of all if you consider yourself AI-savvy — so the instrument you'd use to decide "do I need to check this?" reads high exactly when it should read low. The defense (supervise the output) depends on a faculty (calibrated self-monitoring) that the act of using the tool seems to erode. **This is the book's hardest problem, and this chapter is where it is stated without softening: the thing that saves you is the thing the tool quietly degrades.**

There is a name for the faculty under attack, and it's worth having. The developmental psychologist John Flavell coined **metacognition** in 1979 — "cognition about cognition," the knowing of what you know and the monitoring of your own thinking as it happens. Everything in this chapter is applied metacognition: supervisory judgment is just metacognitive monitoring pointed at an AI's output and, crucially, at your own confidence about that output. When Fernandes finds that AI use degrades the *accuracy* of self-assessment, that is a finding about metacognition specifically — the second-order skill of judging your own first-order judgment. And that is the skill the whole edifice of "keep a human in the loop" silently assumes is intact. The disturbing possibility this chapter forces you to sit with is that the loop can be staffed by a human whose monitoring instrument has quietly drifted, and who therefore signs off with complete sincerity on work they have not actually checked.

It helps to see how the same trap wears different clothes across fields, because the surface looks different each time and the mechanism is identical. In **medicine**, a clinician accepts an AI diagnostic suggestion that is confidently wrong on an atypical presentation — a commission error — and the polish of the output suppresses the second look the unusual case most needed. In **finance**, an analyst signs off on an AI-built model whose assumptions are plausible-sounding but unverified; the calibration failure is invisible until the model is stressed by conditions the assumptions never covered. In **software**, a developer merges AI code that compiles cleanly and reads well but carries a subtle logic error — the "trust trap" of fluent-but-wrong work. In **consulting**, a deck with an internally coherent, confidently argued narrative rests on a misread of the client's actual constraint, and its very coherence is what stops anyone from poking it. In each case the fluency is doing the same job: manufacturing the *signal* of correctness while the *substance* of correctness is absent, and disarming the check by removing the feeling that a check is owed.

## What it means for you

Three implications, in order of how much they should unsettle you.

First: *expertise does not exempt you.* The comforting prior — "I'm senior, I'll catch the errors" — is precisely what the Fernandes literacy result attacks. The more fluent you are with these tools, the more at home you feel in their output, and home is where you stop looking for the unlocked door. The radiologist who has integrated CAD into every read, the analyst who builds every model alongside an assistant, the developer who lives in autocomplete — these are not the safe cases. On the calibration question they are, plausibly, the exposed ones. Treat your own confidence as an unreliable instrument, especially when it is high. [Medium-High]

Second: *the failure is silent.* A miscalibrated checker doesn't feel miscalibrated. The brief felt finished. The model felt right. The diagnosis felt confirmed. There is no internal alarm, because the alarm *is* the calibration that has drifted. This is why the answer cannot be "try harder to be careful." Carefulness that runs on a broken gauge produces confident carelessness.

Third — and this is the hopeful turn — *calibration responds to design, not willpower.* Von Zahn shows it moves with how information is presented. Alter shows it moves when difficulty is induced. Skitka and Mosier show something you can use directly: participants who believed they were **accountable** for the outcome verified the automation more and made fewer commission errors [High]. Calibration is not a fixed trait you either have or lack. It is a behavior you can structure your environment to produce. The defense is not "be a better judge." It is "build a workflow that forces the check your gauge won't ask for."

## The move: predict before you check, rate confidence twice

Here is the concrete habit. It is small, it is fast, and it is designed to manufacture the one thing Fernandes shows you lack — an *externally anchored* baseline that exists before the AI's fluency gets to colonize your judgment.

**Before you read the AI's output, produce your own.** Not the full answer — a prediction. On a contract clause: what do I expect this to say, and where do I expect the risk to be? On a root-cause analysis: what would I guess is going on? On a piece of code: what should this function do, and where would I expect it to break? Write it down. It takes thirty seconds and it costs you the ability to be retroactively sure you "knew that all along."

**Then rate your confidence — twice.** Before you check the AI against reality, write a number: how confident am I that this output is correct, 0 to 100? Then do the actual check — run the code, pull the cited case, re-derive the number, call the one person who'd know. Then write a second number: how confident am I now? The gap between the two is your personal calibration data, accumulating in real time. Most people are startled, the first dozen times, by how often the first number was high and the check changed the answer.

Why this works, mechanically: the prediction creates a baseline the AI's fluency cannot overwrite, so you are comparing two answers rather than judging one persuasive one. The confidence ratings convert your invisible, drifting gauge into a visible, logged one — you can no longer *not notice* that you were 90% sure and wrong. And the deliberate pause-to-check is itself the induced difficulty that the disfluency research says summons System 2. You are not relying on feeling careful. You are building a gate that the feeling can't talk you out of.

Do this on the work that has stakes — the deliverable someone will rely on, the number that drives a decision, the claim you'll have to defend. You don't need to gate everything. You need to stop letting fluency decide which things get checked.

A predictable objection: this feels like busywork, especially when the AI is right most of the time, which it is. Two answers. First, the cost is asymmetric. The thirty-second prediction is cheap; the fluent-but-wrong output you ship unchecked can be expensive in exactly the way the fabricated citation was expensive — invisible until it is catastrophic, and catastrophic precisely on the cases that mattered enough to be unusual. You are not buying down average error; you are buying down the rare, high-cost error that fluency is best at hiding. Second, and this is the part most people miss: the confidence log is *training data about yourself*. After a few weeks you will know your own calibration — the domains where your "90%" means 90% and the domains where it means 60% — and that knowledge is the calibrated instrument Fernandes shows AI use otherwise erodes. You are not just catching this error. You are rebuilding the gauge.

A note for managers, because the trap scales. If your team's reward structure prizes speed of output above all, you are selecting for passive acceptance and against the predict-then-check habit, and you will get exactly the silent, confident, fluent errors the structure incentivizes. The cheap institutional fix is the one Skitka and Mosier point to: make accountability concrete. The person who knows they will have to defend the number checks the number. The person who can say "the AI produced it" does not.

If you want a picture to hold onto, picture a calibration curve: confidence on one axis, actual accuracy on the other, with a diagonal line marking perfect calibration — where being 80% sure means you're right 80% of the time. A well-calibrated judge hugs that diagonal. The Fernandes result is, in effect, a curve that has slid *below* the diagonal: high confidence, lower accuracy, the overconfidence gap visible as the distance between where you sit and where the line says you should. The predict-and-rate habit is a machine for pulling your curve back toward the diagonal — every logged "I was 90% sure and wrong" is a data point that drags your future 90% closer to honesty. You cannot fix a curve you cannot see, and the whole point of the move is to make yours visible.

## Bridge

Supervision catches the wrong answer. But catching it well requires more than a calibrated gauge — it requires being able to reconstruct *why* the answer is wrong, which usually means tracing cause and effect. And here the machine's fluency hides a second, deeper limitation: it can narrate a causal story without performing any causal reasoning at all. That is the next chapter.
