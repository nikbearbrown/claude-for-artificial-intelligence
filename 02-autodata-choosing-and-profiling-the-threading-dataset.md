# Chapter 2 — AutoData: Choosing and Profiling the Threading Dataset

## The first thing you should do, and almost no one does

A new dataset lands in your inbox. The natural instinct — the one every tutorial reinforces — is to load it and start building. Read the CSV, split into train and test, fit a model, look at the score. You can be modeling within ten minutes.

Resist it.

The first thing you should do with a dataset is not model it. It is *interrogate its fitness for purpose.* A dataset is not "good" or "bad" in the abstract; it is **fit or unfit for a specific job** (Sambasivan et al., 2021; settled view). [High] The job in this book is to teach data cleaning end to end, which means the dataset has to actually *contain* every kind of mess we want to teach. The job in your future will be different — predict default, forecast demand, flag fraud — but the question is the same: does this data have what my task requires, in a form I can trust?

So before we clean anything, we profile. And before we even profile, we score the LendingClub data against an explicit specification — a thirteen-property scorecard — so we know, with evidence, whether this is the right file to thread through the whole book. This is the chapter where you learn to look before you leap, and to write down what you saw.

## What we are choosing, and why LendingClub

The threading dataset for *GIGO: AutoData* is **LendingClub loan data**: hundreds of thousands of peer-to-peer consumer loans, each row an application plus its eventual outcome, each column an attribute of the borrower, the loan, or the credit profile. [verify against the actual LendingClub file] It is public, downloadable without a login, and large enough to feel real while still running on a laptop.

It earns its place for reasons beyond size. It is *operational* data — generated by a real lending business, not assembled by a textbook author — so its defects are the authentic residue of how lending data actually gets recorded. It spans the domains a first-job data scientist plausibly works in (finance, risk, credit). And critically, it is messy in *natural* ways: the interest rate is stored as a string with a percent sign because that is how it was displayed on a web page; income has wild outliers because borrowers self-report it; a delinquency-history column is mostly blank because most borrowers have never been delinquent. Nobody injected these problems. They are the fingerprints of the real world.

> **A note on candidate datasets.** We considered alternatives — the CFPB Consumer Complaint Database (strong on text, weaker on a clean numeric target), UCI Online Retail (excellent for outliers and skew, awkward for a binary target), and IBM-style HR attrition data (familiar target, but most public versions are too clean or frankly synthetic). LendingClub won because it scores highest against the full scorecard *with real, undocumented messiness intact.* We record this comparison so the choice is auditable, not assumed.

## The scorecard: a fitness test you can defend

Here is the specification the dataset has to satisfy. We need thirteen properties present *naturally* in the raw data, because the book's credibility rests on you recognizing "yes, this is what real data looks like." The table below is the **AutoData scorecard** — the recurring artifact of this book. We fill it in with evidence, mark every precise statistic as needing verification against the actual file, and carry an updated version of it through every subsequent chapter.

[Medium — pending the verification pass below.] That is a strong result and the reason we commit to it for the whole book.

> **Disclosure, stated once and clearly.** Where a property is thin in a particular sampled slice — most plausibly the free-text `desc` column, which LendingClub deprecated for newer loans and which is blank for many rows — we may *deepen* an existing real feature for teaching, or note that we work with the subset of loans where the text exists. Any such intervention is marked with a ⚠ symbol at the point of use. We never artificially manufacture the class imbalance, the ambiguous outliers, or the core missingness pattern — those must be, and are, real (per the book's injection policy).

## Profiling, the AutoData way

Scoring tells you *whether* to use the dataset. Profiling tells you *what is in it.* In the AutoData workflow you drive this with an assistant, but — as always — you read every result and draw every conclusion yourself.

Start with the cheapest, most informative move in all of data science: look at the shape, the types, and the first few rows.

```python
import pandas as pd

# A working subset of columns keeps the profile legible (property #2).
cols = [
    "loan_status", "loan_amnt", "int_rate", "term", "grade", "sub_grade",
    "emp_title", "emp_length", "home_ownership", "annual_inc", "purpose",
    "title", "desc", "revol_bal", "revol_util",
    "mths_since_last_delinq", "issue_d", "earliest_cr_line",
]
loans = pd.read_csv("lending_club.csv", usecols=cols, low_memory=False)

print(loans.shape)          # rows, columns
print(loans.dtypes)         # which columns came in as object (string)?
loans.head()
```

The `dtypes` output is where the first surprises live. Columns you *expect* to be numeric — `int_rate`, `term`, `revol_util` — come back as `object`, which is pandas's way of saying "string." [Medium / current pandas behavior] That single fact tells you property #6 is satisfied and warns you that any arithmetic on those columns will fail or, worse, silently do the wrong thing.

Next, quantify missingness, because property #3 and #4 hinge on it:

```python
missing = loans.isna().mean().sort_values(ascending=False)
print((missing[missing > 0] * 100).round(1))
```

You are looking for at least three columns above 5% missing, with at least two different mechanisms. The output will likely show `mths_since_last_delinq` and `desc` very high, with `emp_title`, `emp_length`, and `revol_util` lower but nonzero. [verify against the actual LendingClub file] We do not diagnose the *mechanisms* yet — that is Chapter 3's entire job — but we confirm the raw material is here.

Now the categoricals, to verify cardinality (properties #8, #9, #10):

```python
for c in ["home_ownership", "purpose", "grade", "emp_title"]:
    n = loans[c].nunique(dropna=True)
    print(f"{c:15s} {n:>6} unique values")
```

You expect `home_ownership` and `purpose` to be low-cardinality nominal (a handful of values), `grade` to be low-cardinality but *ordinal* (A through G have an order — A is the best credit grade), and `emp_title` to be high-cardinality nominal with thousands of distinct, messily-capitalized strings. [verify against the actual LendingClub file] The contrast between `grade` and `emp_title` is the whole point of Chapter 7: same data type, completely different correct treatment.

Finally, look at the numerics that will need transforming (property #11):

```python
print(loans[["annual_inc", "revol_bal", "loan_amnt"]].describe())
print("skew:\n", loans[["annual_inc", "revol_bal", "loan_amnt"]].skew(numeric_only=True))
```

A right-skewed column betrays itself two ways: the mean sits well above the median, and the skewness statistic exceeds 1. [Medium] You expect `annual_inc` and `revol_bal` to be strongly right-skewed — a few very large values dragging a long tail — which is exactly the shape that motivates the log transforms of Chapter 8 and the outlier judgment of Chapter 4.

### The trap of the numeric-looking categorical

One profiling check saves more downstream grief than its modesty suggests: ask whether a column that *looks* numeric actually *is* numeric. LendingClub carries a `zip_code` field (stored as a three-digit prefix like `100xx`) and various ID columns. [verify against the actual LendingClub file] If a column like that slips into the file as an integer, `describe()` will happily report its mean and standard deviation — numbers that are not merely useless but actively misleading. The "average zip code" is meaningless; the difference between zip `100` and zip `200` is not twice anything. A model handed zip as a number will invent a fictional ordering and learn fictional patterns from it.

The diagnostic is to look, not to trust the dtype:

```python
for c in ["zip_code", "grade", "term"]:
    print(c, "->", loans[c].dropna().unique()[:8])
```

If the "numbers" are really labels — discrete codes with no meaningful arithmetic between them — the column is categorical wearing a numeric costume, and you flag it for treatment as a category in Chapter 7. This is a small check that prevents a whole class of silent GIGO failures, and it is exactly the kind of thing an assistant will *not* warn you about unless you ask, because to pandas an integer is an integer.

## Reading the profile like a data scientist, not a tourist

The code above produces numbers. Numbers are not conclusions. The skill this chapter builds is turning a profile into a set of *questions about the world* — the judgment layer, applied at the very start.

When you see `annual_inc` with a maximum in the millions and a mean far above its median, the tourist says "outliers, I'll cap them." The data scientist asks: *is this population one where seven-figure incomes are plausible?* For a consumer-lending platform, a handful of genuinely high earners is believable; a value of exactly `9,500,000.00` next to a `$10,000` loan is suspicious. You do not resolve this now. You *flag* it for Chapter 4, where the resolution requires domain judgment no profile can supply.

When you see `mths_since_last_delinq` missing for most rows, the tourist says "high missingness, maybe drop the column." The data scientist asks: *what does blank mean here?* The most likely answer — that blank means "this borrower has no recorded delinquency" — would make the missingness *informative* and dropping the column an act of throwing away signal. Again, you flag it; Chapter 3 diagnoses it.

This is the discipline: **a profile generates questions, not answers.** The assistant can compute every statistic in seconds. It cannot tell you that a blank in a delinquency column probably means "never delinquent." That inference comes from understanding what the column is *about*, and it is yours.

### Profiling is provenance work, not just statistics

There is a temptation to treat profiling as a checklist of summary statistics — run `describe()`, eyeball the output, move on. That is the tourist's profile. The data scientist's profile is also an act of *provenance reconstruction*: using the shape of the data to infer how it was produced, because how it was produced determines what it can be trusted to say.

Consider what the `int_rate` string format alone tells you. The fact that interest rate arrives as `"13.49%"` — a human-readable display string with a trailing percent sign — strongly implies the data was scraped or exported from an interface built for people to read, not for machines to compute on. That single inference predicts other defects: dates may be in display formats, currency may carry symbols, categories may be capitalized for presentation rather than for storage. One column's format is a clue to the whole file's lineage. Reading data this way — as evidence about a process — is the habit Chapter 1 borrowed from Deming and that Gebru and colleagues (2021) formalized as the *datasheet*: a dataset should travel with a record of where it came from, who made it, and for what. When that record is missing, profiling is how you reconstruct as much of it as the data will surrender.

This is why the AutoData scorecard has a column for *what remains unknown.* A profile that only records what you found, and not what you still cannot determine, gives a false sense of completeness. The honest scorecard says, for `desc`: "blank for most rows; cause inferred to be field deprecation by loan vintage; **not yet confirmed against issue-date.**" That last clause is the difference between a profile and a guess dressed as a profile.

## Before and after: from a file to a characterized dataset

Here is the transformation this chapter delivers, and it is not a transformation of the data at all — it is a transformation of *what you know.*

**Before:** an opaque CSV named `lending_club.csv`. You know its filename. You assume it is loans. You are tempted to model.

**After:** a *characterized* dataset. You know it has the rows and columns the book needs; you know `int_rate` and `term` are strings that must be parsed; you know `annual_inc` and `revol_bal` are right-skewed with ambiguous extremes; you know `mths_since_last_delinq` is mostly blank and that the blank is probably informative; you know `emp_title` is a high-cardinality mess and `grade` is a clean ordinal; you know the target is imbalanced roughly 1-in-8. And you have it all written down in a scorecard you can hand to the next person.

You have not improved the data by a single value. You have made it *legible* — and legibility is the precondition for every cleaning decision still to come. The AutoData scorecard you started here is the document that will accumulate, row by row, the entire history of how this file became trustworthy.

## The AI Wayback Machine: Hadley Wickham

> When you decide that `loan_status`, `annual_inc`, and `int_rate` should each be a column, that every loan should be a row, and that each cell holds one value, you are not inventing that structure — you are applying a principle that **Hadley Wickham** crystallized in his 2014 paper *Tidy Data*. Wickham, a statistician and the architect of much of the modern R data-analysis ecosystem, gave the field a precise, almost deceptively simple definition of clean tabular structure: **each variable forms a column, each observation forms a row, each type of observational unit forms a table** (Wickham, 2014). [High]
>
> The power of the idea is that it turns a vague aesthetic preference — "the data should be tidy" — into a checkable specification. When you profile LendingClub and notice that `term` smuggles a unit (" months") into its values, or that a single conceptual variable might be split across columns, you are detecting violations of Wickham's rules. Tidy Data gives this chapter its answer to the question *clean into what shape?* You are not just removing mess; you are reshaping the file toward a structure where one column means one thing. Every profiling move in this chapter — checking dtypes, counting unique values, separating units from numbers — is a step toward Wickham's tidy ideal, and every later chapter assumes you are aiming there.
