# Introduction

In 1906, a single-celled organism solved a maze.

Not metaphorically. Literally. Toshiyuki Nakagaki placed a slime mold at the entrance of a plastic labyrinth, put food at the exit, and watched. The organism — yellow, spreading, brainless — explored every passage and every dead end. Then it began to prune. Within hours, it had concentrated its entire body into one tube: the shortest possible path. No neurons. No plan. No map.

The question this book takes seriously is the one that moment forces: what do we actually mean when we say a thing is intelligent?

Because the slime mold clearly did something. And the word we reach for, instinctively, is the wrong word — or at least a word we have never properly defined. This is the problem the book begins with and never entirely lets go of. Intelligence is not a thing. It is a family of questions — about sensing and deciding, about learning and remembering, about feeling and planning and imagining your own future — and the questions turn out to be far older, and far stranger, than the brain.

This book takes you through that family one organism at a time.

We start before brains, with bacteria navigating chemical gradients using two enzymes and a four-second memory. We meet the nematode *C. elegans* — one millimeter long, 302 neurons, every synapse documented — and watch it do something a smoke detector cannot: change its behavior based on an internal state it did not choose. We follow the sea slug *Aplysia* into a Nobel laureate's laboratory in New York, where it turned out to carry the molecular mechanism of memory that every animal from slug to human shares. We watch fish in aquariums recognize faces, rats in mazes run through maps they've never been given, bees navigate cities, and chimpanzees in Japanese research centers demonstrate an understanding of what another mind knows.

At the end, we reach language models — AI systems that produce fluent text, pass professional examinations, and exhibit behaviors that no one fully predicted from their architecture. Whether any of this constitutes intelligence depends entirely on which definition you brought to the question. This book is about how to hold that question well.

The argument running through every chapter is this: intelligence, whatever it is, is not the exclusive property of the brain, or of vertebrates, or of language-using creatures. It appears in pieces, in older animals, in simpler substrates, distributed across evolutionary time in ways that suggest it is less a single invention than a set of solutions that keep getting rediscovered. The bacterium solved the navigation problem two billion years ago. Every more elaborate cognitive system built since is running the same ancient logic, faster and with more parameters.

You will not leave this book with a single clean definition. Twenty-four researchers were asked for theirs in 1986 and produced twenty-four that didn't fit together — that story opens Chapter One, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. What you will leave with is something more useful: a way of asking the question precisely enough that it can be answered, one organism at a time, starting with the simplest version of the problem and working toward the ones that still don't have answers.

The slime mold is waiting at the entrance of the maze. Let's follow it in.
