# Training the Durable Capacities

*Judgment is trainable — but only under specific conditions the average "AI training" ignores.*

It's a Thursday, and your company has booked the vendor workshop. Everyone files into a conference room or a Zoom grid. A trainer walks through the AI tool's interface, demonstrates a few impressive prompts, shares a slide deck of "prompt patterns," and hands out a one-page cheat sheet. People nod. Someone asks about data privacy. The session ends on time. There is a feedback form, and the scores are good — it was clear, it was useful, the trainer was friendly.

Three weeks later, nothing about how anyone actually works has changed. The cheat sheet is in a drawer. The capabilities the company hoped to build — better judgment about when to trust the tool, sharper problem framing, the ability to catch a fluent-but-wrong output — are exactly where they were. The workshop trained nothing, because a one-day demonstration meets none of the conditions under which human capacities actually improve.

This is not a knock on trainers. It is a structural fact about how expertise is built, and it has been studied for forty years. This chapter is about those conditions — what real training of judgment requires, why most "AI training" fails to provide it, and how you can build the conditions into your own working week. The previous chapter gave you gates to keep your judgment *engaged*. This one is about making it *grow*.

## The evidence: what makes practice work

The modern science of expertise begins with a 1993 paper by K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Römer, "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance." Their claim was that expert performance is not mainly a gift but a product — built by a specific kind of practice. *Deliberate practice* is individualized, pitched at the edge of your current ability, rich with immediate feedback, repetitive on the hard parts, and usually guided by a coach who can see what you can't. Crucially, it resists automaticity: it keeps the learner in the effortful, attention-demanding "cognitive stage" rather than letting the skill go on autopilot. Ericsson argued, famously, that differences in expert performance could "largely be accounted for by differential amounts of past and current levels of practice." [High as a framework]

That framework spawned the popular "10,000-hour rule" — which Ericsson himself disavowed, because it dropped the words that mattered. The hours were never the point. The *quality and structure* of the hours were the point. A workshop is hours of exposure. Exposure is precisely what deliberate practice is not.

Now the honest counterweight, because this chapter would be propaganda without it. In 2014, Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick, and Frederick Oswald published a meta-analysis testing how much of the variance in performance deliberate practice actually explains. The answer was: a meaningful slice, but far less than the strong version of the theory implies — and it varied wildly by domain. Deliberate practice explained about 26% of the variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and **less than 1% in professions.** [High] Read that last number against this book's audience. In professional work — the domain you live in — the measured share of performance attributable to structured practice is small.

There is a rebuttal to the rebuttal, and it deserves a fair hearing. A 2021 paper in *Current Psychology* argued that the meta-analyses *understate* the effect because they pooled studies that did not actually measure *individualized* practice — the defining feature of the whole theory. In a study of chess players, when practice was both individualized and high-quality, its effect was more than three times larger than at average levels. [Medium — single follow-up study; reframes the debate rather than settling it. Confirm the first author's name before citing.]

So where does the honest reader land? Three positions, all on the table: deliberate practice explains most of expertise (Ericsson); it explains a minority and almost nothing in professions (Macnamara and Hambrick); the studies mismeasured the key ingredient and understate it (the individualization camp). The defensible synthesis is this: structured, feedback-rich, individualized practice is *necessary and trainable*, but it is *not sufficient*, and in professions specifically its measured share is small. Training judgment is worth doing. It is not a magic wand. Anyone who sells it to you as one is doing the laundering this book exists to warn against.

## A method built for people with no time to practice

Here is the practical problem. Deliberate practice in the classic sense — a violinist working scales with a teacher, a chess player drilling endgames — assumes time set aside for practice as a separate activity. The working professional has no such time. The meeting is the meeting; the deliverable is due; there is no rehearsal room.

Peter Fadde and Gary Klein addressed exactly this in a 2010 paper, "Deliberate Performance: Accelerating Expertise in Natural Settings." Their insight: if you can't carve out separate practice, embed the learning *into the work itself* through four exercises — the four E's. [Medium — theoretically sound, sparsely tested directly]

- **Estimation.** Before you act, forecast the outcome. Predict the number, the result, the reaction. This externalizes your mental model and — just as importantly — creates a baseline you can later check against reality. (You'll recognize this as the numeracy-baseline gate from the last chapter, now repurposed as a *training* device rather than a checkpoint.)
- **Experimentation.** Introduce small, controlled variations in how you do something, on purpose, to see what changes. Vary the method, not just the task, so causal pathways reveal themselves.
- **Extrapolation.** Mine your surprises and near-failures for structural lessons. When something didn't go as predicted, don't just file it — ask what general principle the surprise just taught you.
- **Explanation.** Articulate your reasoning out loud, to yourself or a colleague, *after* the fact. Tacit reasoning that is never spoken never gets examined or corrected.

Walk one of them through a real moment. Say you're about to send a client a recommendation that an AI helped you draft. *Estimation* says: before you read the AI's version, write down what you expect the client's reaction and the actual outcome to be — will they accept it, push back, ask for what? *Experimentation* says: next time, vary one thing on purpose — frame the same recommendation two ways and watch which lands. *Extrapolation* says: when the client reacts in a way you didn't predict, don't just absorb it — ask what general lesson about this client, or this kind of recommendation, the surprise just handed you. *Explanation* says: afterward, articulate to a colleague *why* you made the call you did, because the act of saying it out loud is what turns a tacit hunch into examinable, improvable reasoning. None of that required a single hour outside the work. It required attaching a learning loop to work that was happening anyway.

The four E's are valuable precisely because they require no extra hours. They convert the work you already do into reps. But hold the confidence honestly: whether deliberate performance *measurably* accelerates expertise in real workplaces is theoretically motivated and sparsely tested. [Medium] Use it as a well-reasoned structure, not a proven protocol.

## The target: adaptive, not routine

Why train at all, if AI is so capable? Because of a distinction drawn by two Japanese psychologists, Giyoo Hatano and Kayoko Inagaki, in 1986. They split expertise in two. The *routine expert* is fast, accurate, and automated within familiar situations — but inflexible when the situation shifts. The *adaptive expert* uses deep conceptual understanding to handle novel situations, to predict, and to invent new procedures when the old ones don't fit. [High, as a conceptual distinction]

This is the chapter's spine, and arguably the most load-bearing idea in the book. **AI replicates routine expertise.** It is superb at the fast, accurate, pattern-matched response within a familiar frame — exactly the routine expert's home turf. What it does not perform is genuine adaptation to the novel, the underspecified, the situation no training data covered. Adaptive expertise is therefore precisely the capacity that survives, and it is the training target.

Hatano and Inagaki also named the conditions that grow adaptive expertise, which double as a training prescription: (1) a *variable or random context* that forces you to adapt rather than repeat; (2) a *safe environment* where rewards don't hinge on every single performance, so you can take the risks that learning requires; and (3) a *culture that values understanding over efficiency*. Notice that the typical AI-integrated workplace inverts all three — it standardizes context, raises the stakes on every output, and prizes speed over comprehension. The conditions for adaptive expertise are not the default. They have to be built on purpose.

## The mechanism — and the AI-era break

The last foundational piece explains *how* judgment was traditionally transmitted, and why AI threatens the transmission. In 1989, Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, and Susan Newman described *cognitive apprenticeship*: the observation that expert thinking is largely *invisible* during normal work. The expert produces the artifact — the brief, the diagnosis, the design — but the reasoning that produced it stays in their head. Apprenticeship, they argued, works only when that invisible reasoning is deliberately made visible, through six methods: **modeling** (showing the thinking), **coaching**, **scaffolding** (supports that fade as the learner grows), **articulation** (the learner explains), **reflection**, and **exploration**. [High]

This model has been tested, and it works. A recent randomized clinical trial applied cognitive apprenticeship to the training of critical-care residents and found improved critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and decision-making compared to controls. [Medium-High] Making invisible reasoning visible is not a nice idea; it measurably improves judgment.

Now the AI-era break, which is the hinge connecting this chapter to the next. When AI generates the visible artifact — the code, the draft, the analysis — and the expert merely reviews it, the apprentice never sees the *pre-artifact reasoning*. They never watch the expert decide what to solve, what to reject, which constraints bind, where the real difficulty lives. Modeling, coaching, and scaffolding are all bypassed in one move. The apprentice learns to operate the AI; they do not learn the judgment behind it. This is a strong circumstantial argument, not a directly measured effect — flag it as such. [Medium] But if it holds, it means the same delegation that boosts this quarter's output quietly destroys the channel through which next decade's experts were made. That is precisely the broken ladder of Chapter 11.

## What it means for you

Put the four findings together and a training logic falls out. The *target* is adaptive expertise (Hatano and Inagaki), because that is what AI doesn't perform. The *method* is deliberate, individualized, feedback-rich practice (Ericsson), embedded in real work as the four E's (Fadde and Klein) because you have no separate hours. The *delivery* is cognitive apprenticeship — making reasoning visible (Collins, Brown, and Newman) — which AI delegation otherwise cuts out. And the *honest caveat* is the variance debate (Macnamara and Hambrick): this is worth doing, it is not a guarantee, and in professions the share of performance you can move by training is real but modest.

Here is the reference artifact — the capacities this book argues are durable, the methods with the best evidence behind them, and the conditions each method requires to actually work:

| Durable capacity | Methods with evidence | Conditions required |
|---|---|---|
| **Supervisory judgment / calibration** (Ch. 4) | Confidence ratings before and after feedback; AI-output auditing with explicit error-detection targets; "what would change my mind?" prompts | Immediate feedback on whether your confidence matched reality; stakes that make a wrong call cost something |
| **Causal reasoning** (Ch. 5) | DAG construction and critique; pre-mortems; generating alternative explanations | Adversarial pressure; an expert who can challenge your causal story; real (not toy) problems |
| **Problem formulation** (Ch. 6) | "Reject the prompt" exercises; analyzing poorly-framed problems before solving them | Underspecified, realistic problems; permission to redefine the question |
| **Judgment under pressure** (Ch. 7) | After-action reviews; morbidity & mortality conferences; case method; clinical rounds | Real or simulated stakes; public accountability; a culture that examines failure without punishing honesty |
| **Collective intelligence** (Ch. 7) | Red-team / blue-team; structured debate; steelmanning | Genuine disagreement among participants; safety to dissent |

Every row obeys the same rule: the method only trains the capacity *if* its conditions are met. Confidence ratings train calibration only with immediate feedback. Debate trains collective judgment only with real disagreement. This is why the vendor workshop fails — it provides the activity and none of the conditions.

Look back at that Thursday workshop with this table in hand and the failure is no longer mysterious. It had no individualization — everyone got the same demo regardless of their actual gaps. It had no feedback loop — nobody found out, days later, whether the techniques improved their judgment. It had no stakes — nothing rode on getting it right in the room. And it had no coaching — no expert watching your specific reasoning and correcting it. The workshop scored well on the feedback form because the feedback form measured *clarity and satisfaction*, which are real, and which have nothing to do with whether a capacity got built. This is the trap of confusing the *experience* of learning with the *fact* of it — the same illusion the deskilling research found in passive AI use, where fluency feels like understanding. A good workshop and a fluent AI output share a property: both feel like learning happened. Neither guarantees it did.

## The move

Pick one capacity. One. Not the whole table.

Choose a single durable capacity that matters most in your actual work — say, supervisory judgment if you ship AI-assisted deliverables, or causal reasoning if you diagnose problems for a living. Then pick *one drill* from its row, and **schedule it like training, not like reading.** A specific time, a specific cadence, a specific feedback loop.

A concrete version: if you choose calibration, then for the next two weeks, before you accept any significant AI output, write down a confidence number (0–100) for "this is correct and usable as-is." Then check it against what actually happened — did the output survive review, did it hold up downstream? Once a week, look at your numbers against the outcomes. You are building the one thing the workshop can't give you: a feedback loop on your own judgment. That is deliberate practice, embedded in the work, aimed at the capacity AI can't perform.

Reading about training is not training. The difference between this chapter and a vendor cheat sheet is whether you put one drill on your calendar before you close the book.

## Bridge

You can train your own judgment. But you are one person inside a system that used to manufacture experts at scale — through junior work that doubled as apprenticeship — and that system is now breaking for sound economic reasons.
