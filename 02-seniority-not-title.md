# It's Your Seniority, Not Your Title

*The squeeze falls on the entry level — and it's breaking the ladder that makes experts.*

The memo is two paragraphs long and it does not mention artificial intelligence by name. It is the kind of internal note that circulates every spring: the firm's graduate intake plan for the coming year. Last cycle the number was forty. This cycle it is twenty. The explanation is a single clause — *given productivity gains across the analyst function, we are right-sizing the incoming class.* There is no malice in it. There is no panic in it. There is just a line item, halved.

The forty people who would have been hired into that other twenty slots will not show up as a statistic anywhere. They were never employed, so they cannot be laid off. They simply do not enter. And the partners signing the memo are, individually, behaving rationally: the work those juniors used to do is now partly covered by tools, so why pay for forty when twenty plus the tools will do? Each firm makes this call on its own, quietly, and the calls add up to something none of them decided and none of them are responsible for. That is the shape of the squeeze. It is not a wave. It is a door that stops opening.

## The finding that reframes your risk

The previous chapter sorted the work by *task* — execution versus judgment. This chapter introduces a second axis, and it is the sharper one, because somebody measured it cleanly: the squeeze is falling by *seniority*, not by occupation. The same job, done by a younger and an older worker, is showing opposite outcomes. That is not what "AI takes jobs" predicts. It is something more specific, and worse.

The cleanest measurement comes from Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen, in a 2025 Stanford Digital Economy Lab paper with a title that tells you the authors knew what they had found: *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence*. They used payroll microdata from ADP — the largest U.S. payroll processor, covering more than 25 million workers — which means they were not looking at job ads or surveys but at actual people on actual payrolls.

The headline fact: workers **aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations** have seen a **relative employment decline of about 16% since late 2022**, after controlling for firm-level shocks [High confidence — large administrative dataset, careful identification]. *(An earlier calculation circulated at around 13%; the current paper headlines roughly 16%. Use 16%, and note that the number has already moved once — a live demonstration of why labor figures in this book carry dates.)* Older workers in those *same* occupations were stable or growing. Workers of all ages in *less*-exposed occupations were stable or growing. And the declines clustered where AI **automates** rather than **augments** the work.

Read that again, because it is the chapter's whole argument: a **seniority-level effect, not an occupation-level effect.** Two people can have the same job title, in the same exposed occupation, and the twenty-three-year-old is losing ground while the forty-five-year-old is not. The risk does not live in your occupation. It lives in your *level* within it.

## A second study, a cleaner mechanism

One study is a finding. Two independent studies with different data and the same answer is a pillar. The second is the one this book cites by its corrected name, because the synthesis it came from had it wrong.

Hosseini Maasoum and Lichtinger, *Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change: Evidence from U.S. Résumé and Job Posting Data* (SSRN Working Paper 5425555, 2025), is **not** "the Harvard study," and it is **not** built on administrative records — those are two different mislabelings worth correcting, because they get cited wrongly. It is résumé and job-posting data covering roughly **62 million workers across 285,000 firms** from 2015 to 2025, presented through the Stanford Digital Economy Lab. (Lichtinger's Harvard affiliation is presumably where the "Harvard" tag came from; the administrative-records study is the *Danish* Humlum–Vestergaard paper from the last chapter — a genuinely different study.)

The design is what makes it valuable. The authors identify which firms are actively implementing generative AI by detecting "GenAI integrator" job postings — a firm signaling, in its own hiring, that it is putting these tools to work — and then compare adopting firms to non-adopting firms over time, a difference-in-differences design. The comparison is the point: it isolates what changes *because of adoption*, holding the broader economy roughly constant across both groups.

The result: after a firm adopts, **junior employment declines sharply relative to non-adopters** — the synthesis puts the magnitude at roughly **7–12% within six quarters** — while **senior employment continues to rise**. The decline concentrates in the most GenAI-exposed occupations, begins around the first quarter of 2023, and is driven by **slower hiring, not layoffs or separations** [High confidence — large sample, well-identified design]. It even has a texture worth noting: graduates of mid-tier institutions are hit hardest, while graduates of the very-highest- and lowest-prestige institutions are less affected.

One claim from the secondary synthesis does *not* survive contact with the paper. It was said that internal promotions rose slightly under adoption. They did not: the paper finds the junior decline is driven by *slower hiring* and is explicitly **not** explained by increased separations *or* promotions — promotions are among the channels the authors rule out, not one that grew [High confidence — verified against the working paper]. The defensible core is the seniority-biased decline through reduced entry, and that core is solid.

## It is not only tech, and it is not only the U.S.

If the only evidence were two studies of AI-exposed occupations, a skeptic could call it a tech story. It is broader than that.

In tech itself, SignalFire's 2025 *State of Tech Talent Report* finds new-graduate hiring at the "Magnificent Seven" largest firms down **more than 50% since 2022**, entry-level hiring at the fifteen biggest tech firms down **25% from 2023 to 2024** — and, tellingly, mid-career hiring (two to five years of experience) **up 27%** over a comparable window. That mid-career *rise* alongside the entry-level *fall* is the seniority effect in a single dataset. The same report carries a line that is less a statistic than a confession: **37% of managers say they would rather use AI than hire a Gen Z employee** [Medium confidence — industry report, reliable for direction, not peer-reviewed; date-stamp 2025].

Beyond tech, and beyond the U.S.: Adzuna's UK data shows **entry-level jobs down roughly 32% since the November 2022 launch of ChatGPT**, with junior developer roles especially thin — around 100 advertised in the six months to March 2025, against about 312 a year earlier [Medium confidence — industry data]. *(A figure of −46% circulated in earlier synthesis; current Adzuna reporting supports roughly 32%, so 32% is the number used here, and the −46% is set aside as unconfirmed.)* And finance, from the last chapter, is the same shape from the employer's side: the Oliver Wyman/NYSE "diamond," 64% of CFOs shifting away from junior roles.

Different sectors, different countries, different data sources, one direction: the entry point is narrowing. The magnitudes vary and the figures are perishable — all of them age within six to twelve months, so treat the *direction* as well-established and any single *number* as a snapshot.

## The mechanism: the ladder was free, and now it isn't

So far this is a finding about hiring. The reason it matters for a whole career — yours, and the careers of the people you will one day need to promote — is a mechanism that takes a moment to see clearly, because it is structural and almost invisible while it works.

How did anyone ever become a senior expert? Not by being hired as one. They were hired as juniors, given the routine, executable, lower-stakes work — the document review, the reconciliation, the first-draft analysis, the boilerplate — and over years of doing it, *watched* and *corrected* by the people above them, they built judgment. The apprenticeship was not a separate program. It was a **byproduct** of the junior doing real production work. The firm got the work done *and* manufactured its next generation of seniors, and the second part was nearly free, because it rode along on the first.

Generative AI breaks this in a precise way. The routine work that *was* the apprenticeship curriculum is exactly the work AI now does cheaply. So the firm can get that production done without hiring the junior — which means it also stops, as a side effect, manufacturing the senior. Training, which used to be a costless byproduct of junior production, becomes an **explicit, separable cost**: if you want a trained junior now, you have to *pay* for the training as its own line item, because it no longer rides along on work you needed done anyway.

And here the economics turn against everyone. This is Gary Becker's old problem about general human capital, now triggered by AI. A firm that pays to train a junior in transferable judgment creates an asset that can walk out the door to a competitor who paid nothing — the **poaching externality**. When training was a free byproduct, this did not bite; the firm trained because it was getting the work regardless. Now that training is a separable cost with a poaching risk attached, the rational move for each individual firm is to **under-invest** — to hire the already-trained mid-career worker that *someone else* paid to develop, and skip the expensive, leaky business of growing one. Every firm reasoning this way is reasoning correctly. The collective result is that the ladder stops being built, and no single firm is responsible, because each one was just declining to subsidize its competitors. This is a textbook market failure: individually rational, collectively ruinous [Medium-High confidence — the mechanism is well-grounded in human-capital theory and consistent with the hiring data; what is documented is the reduced entry, not yet the downstream shortage].

## The inference the data does not yet support

Follow the mechanism forward and it points somewhere alarming: if firms stop hiring and training juniors today, then in five to ten years the pipeline that refills the senior ranks runs dry, and the same firms that are flush with senior talent now face a **shortage** of it later — having eaten their seed corn.

This is logical. It follows cleanly from the mechanism. And it is, as of writing, **not directly evidenced** — no one has yet observed the senior shortage, because not enough time has passed [Low-Medium confidence]. I state it as what it is: a reasoned projection from a documented mechanism, not a measured fact. It is exactly the kind of claim this book refuses to launder into certainty. The hiring decline is real and measured. The downstream shortage is a forecast — plausible, worth acting on, but a forecast. Keep the two clearly separated in your own head, because the people selling you AI strategy will not.

## What it means for you

If you are early-career, this chapter is the bad news delivered straight: the door is narrower than it was for the people one rung above you, and it narrowed for reasons that have nothing to do with you personally. But notice precisely what the data does and does not say. It says *entry* is contracting through reduced hiring. It does **not** say juniors are being fired, and it does **not** say senior and mid-career demand is falling — those are *rising*. So the path up still exists; it is just no longer handed to you as a byproduct of getting hired. You will have to build the apprenticeship the old system used to provide for free.

If you are mid-career, the temptation is to exhale — the squeeze is below you, not on you. Resist it, for two reasons. First, the ladder break threatens *your* future: the promotions you are counting on assume a pipeline of people coming up behind you, and the talent you will need to lead is the talent the firm is currently not developing. Second, if you manage anyone, the costless-byproduct era is over on your watch, and whether the ladder gets rebuilt is partly your decision. The mid-career rise in the data is real, but it is not a permanent moat; it is the thickening middle of a diamond whose base is being starved.

## The move: engineer the apprenticeship

The action splits by where you sit, because the structural problem is the same but your lever on it is different.

**If you are junior:** the apprenticeship will not be handed to you, so manufacture your own reps. Concretely — volunteer for the judgment-heavy, non-routine work even when the routine work would be faster and safer; the routine work is the part the tools will do, so winning it teaches you nothing the market will pay for. Find a human model — someone whose judgment you can watch up close — and watch the *reasoning*, not just the output, because the reasoning is the thing AI hides and the thing the old apprenticeship transmitted. When you use AI, use it on the execution half and reserve the judgment half for yourself to struggle through, even when it is slower, because the struggle is the rep. You are deliberately reconstructing, by hand, the training the pyramid used to provide automatically. (Chapters 9 and 10 turn this into a method.)

**If you are senior or a manager:** protect the ladder, knowing it is now a cost you have to choose to pay. Make your expert reasoning *visible* to juniors — narrate the calls you make, not just the conclusions, because the artifact is now cheap and the reasoning behind it is the scarce thing. Defend some quantity of judgment-bearing work for junior hands even when AI could do it faster, treating it as training investment, not inefficiency. Recognize the poaching externality for what it is and decide, deliberately, not to free-ride on it — because if everyone free-rides, the talent you will need in five years is the talent nobody is building today. (Chapter 11 is the full playbook.)

For both: the era when becoming an expert was a free side effect of being employed is ending. From here, building judgment is something you have to *intend*. That is harder. It is also entirely doable, which is the rest of this book.

## Bridge

We now have two axes of the sort: by task (execution versus judgment) and by seniority (the entry-level squeeze). Both point to the same conclusion — your safety lives in the judgment-bearing, hard-to-specify, context-bound work, and your exposure lives in the routine and executable. But "execution versus judgment" is still a coarse instrument. The next chapter sharpens it into a personal tool: the exposure map. We will find that the frontier between what AI does well and what it does badly is not a clean line through your job — it is *jagged*, cutting unpredictably across your tasks — and that the most dangerous moment is not when AI fails you, but when it succeeds fluently at something it has gotten wrong.
