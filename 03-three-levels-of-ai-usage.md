# Chapter 3: Three Levels of AI Usage

## A Tuesday

Watch a senior software engineer named Priya get through an ordinary Tuesday, and watch her supervision quietly fall out of step with her work without her noticing.

9:15 a.m. She pastes a function into her AI assistant: "rename these variables for clarity and fix the formatting." It returns clean, renamed code. She glances at it — the names are sensible, the indentation is right — and accepts it. Total scrutiny: four seconds. This is correct. The task was bounded, the output is inspectable at a glance, and a glance is exactly the right amount of supervision. She has not been careless. She has been *calibrated*.

11:40 a.m. She asks the assistant to summarize three RFCs and a vendor's API documentation into a comparison of authentication approaches. It produces a tidy table with claims about rate limits, token lifetimes, and supported flows. She reads it, finds it plausible, and pastes two of its claims into a design doc. She does *not* open the vendor docs to confirm the token-lifetime figure. The output looked finished, so she treated it the way she treated the variable rename — a glance. But this output was not inspectable at a glance; its correctness lived in sources she did not check. The supervision that fit the 9:15 task is now slightly too light for the 11:40 task. She does not feel the shift. The interface is the same. The confidence of the output is the same.

3:20 p.m. The assistant, now in an agentic mode, proposes an architecture for a new service: a particular message queue, a particular consistency model, a particular way of sharding state. The reasoning is articulate and the diagram is clean. Priya is impressed, a little relieved — this was the hard part of her week — and she adopts the framing. She begins implementing *against the problem as the AI has now defined it.* The choice of consistency model silently encodes assumptions about failure tolerance that the AI had no way to know are wrong for her company's regulatory context. She supervises this — the highest-stakes, problem-shaping output of her day — with the same four-second glance she gave the variable rename at 9:15.

Nothing in Priya's Tuesday looks like negligence. She is good at her job. The failure, when it comes in the form of a costly re-architecture three months later, will not be that she used AI at a high level. It will be that her *supervision stayed at one level while her usage climbed through three* — and she never felt the floor move.

## Three levels, three demands

This chapter is the diagnostic. It gives you a vocabulary for the shift Priya missed, by partitioning AI usage into three qualitatively distinct levels — distinguished not by how impressive the tool is, but by *what the output requires of you to be trusted.*

A word on provenance, in the spirit of the book's honesty discipline. This three-level taxonomy — **copy editor, research assistant, supercollaborator** — is my own teaching framework, not a separately published academic model, and I will not dress it as one [High]. Its value is clarity, not empirical pedigree. But it does not float free. It is a deliberate re-cut of a mature scholarly lineage — the *levels of automation* tradition running from Sheridan and Verplank's 1978 ten-level scale through Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens's 2000 model of types and levels of human–automation interaction [High]. Where that tradition graded automation by how much of a *function* the machine performs, my three levels grade by *collaboration depth* — how deeply the AI's output penetrates the problem itself. The older lineage gives the structure; the three levels adapt it to a kind of collaboration the 1978 and 2000 frameworks predate, because models that *co-shape a problem* did not exist then. (If you have come through Botspeak, its Automation / Augmentation / Agency modes map roughly onto these levels — roughly, not cleanly; do not force the correspondence.)

**Level I — the copy editor.** The AI performs a bounded task on material you already have: fix grammar, reformat a table, rename variables, convert a date format. The defining property is that *the output is inspectable at a glance.* You can see correctness directly, against the input sitting right next to it. Supervisory demand: low, and a glance genuinely suffices. Priya's 9:15.

**Level II — the research assistant.** The AI performs a delegated subtask whose correctness lives *outside the interaction* — it summarizes sources, drafts a literature scan, extracts data, compares documents. The defining property is that *the output requires checking against sources you must go and consult.* You cannot see correctness in the output itself; the output is a *claim about* something you have to verify against. Supervisory demand: moderate, and a glance is no longer enough — you have to leave the screen. This is exactly where the Deloitte report and *Mata v. Avianca* failed: Level II output, trusted with Level I scrutiny. Priya's 11:40.

**Level III — the supercollaborator.** The AI reasons alongside you and its output *shapes the problem itself* — it proposes the framing, the architecture, the analytical approach, the way the question is posed. The defining property is that *the output changes what you are even trying to do.* You must now supervise not only the answer but the *framing the answer assumes* — including the assumptions the framing silently imports. Supervisory demand: highest, and it is a different kind of demand, because the thing most needing scrutiny (the framing) is the thing least visible as a discrete claim. Priya's 3:20.

There is a reason Level III deserves a new name rather than a slot on the old automation scale. The levels-of-automation tradition was built for a world in which automation performed a *function* you had already defined — the autopilot held a heading you set, the analysis tool computed a model you specified. The machine did the work; the human owned the problem. The supercollaborator inverts the last part. It participates in defining the problem. When an AI proposes the architecture, it is not executing your framing faster; it is *supplying a framing*, and every framing silently decides what counts as success, what is treated as constraint, what is ignored as noise. That is genuinely new territory — enabled by the agentic and reasoning models of 2024–26 — and it is why the older scale, excellent as it is, does not quite reach it. The thing most needing supervision at Level III is not an output at all. It is a decision about the problem, smuggled in as an answer.

These are not a ladder of virtue. Level III is not "better" than Level I; it is *appropriate to different work.* Using a supercollaborator to rename variables would be absurd overkill; using a glance to vet an architecture is the failure this chapter is about. The levels are task-matched, not ranked. The single most common misconception students bring to this chapter is that higher-level use is itself the risk — that the safe move is to keep AI at Level I. It is not. Refusing Level III where the work calls for it forfeits the collaboration's value and, worse, drives the same use underground into unsupervised habit. The risk was never the level. The risk is always the *gap*.

| Level | What the AI does | What the output requires | Supervisory demand | Failure if under-supervised |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I — Copy editor | Bounded task on material you have | A glance (correctness is visible) | Low | Cosmetic error slips through |
| II — Research assistant | Delegated subtask | Checking against external sources | Moderate | Fabricated/wrong claim laundered into your work |
| III — Supercollaborator | Co-shapes the problem | Supervising the *framing*, not just the answer | High | A wrong problem solved precisely; silent assumptions adopted |

## The mismatch, and why experts are not immune

The danger this chapter names is not a level. It is a *gap* — between the level of the work and the level of the supervision. Priya did Level III work with Level I habits. That specific mismatch is the disease, and it has a name in the literature firmer than my taxonomy provides.

Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich Manzey, in a 2010 synthesis in *Human Factors*, documented two intertwined phenomena: **automation complacency** — under-monitoring automation that *seems* reliable — and **automation bias** — over-trusting its output even against contrary evidence [High]. Their findings are the empirical backbone of this chapter's diagnosis, and they carry two stings.

The first sting: complacency is *driven by apparent reliability.* The better the automation looks, the less we monitor it. This is precisely backwards from what safety requires, and it is exactly Priya's Tuesday — the more fluent and finished the AI's output, the lighter her scrutiny grew, because fluency reads as reliability. As AI gets better, the *pull toward under-supervision gets stronger*, not weaker. The comfort of Chapter 2 returns here wearing a cognitive-psychology coat: the very improvement that should not reduce verification *does* reduce it, because of how human attention responds to apparent competence.

The second sting, the one that disarms the strongest students: complacency appears in **experts as much as novices**, and it is **not overcome by simple practice or by warnings** [High]. You do not age out of this. You do not train out of it. "I'm experienced, I'd catch it" is not a defense; the experienced were studied, and they did not catch it at meaningfully higher rates. This is why the mismatch is a *diagnosable, persistent* condition and not a beginner's stumble — and why a course, not a warning label, is the appropriate response.

There is a longer-term cost the recent literature is beginning to document, and it sharpens the stakes of the mismatch. As reliance on automation deepens, the underlying human skill can *atrophy* — the manual capacity erodes precisely because it is rarely exercised, so that when supervision finally must engage, the supervisor is less equipped than they assume. The de-skilling literature of 2025–26 spans aviation, clinical reasoning, and software engineering, and while individual figures need checking against their primary sources [Low], the direction is consistent: the more the tool carries the work, the more the human's ability to evaluate the work quietly degrades. The mismatch, then, is not static. Left uncorrected, it widens from both ends — the work climbs while the capacity to supervise it sinks. The supercollaborator who shapes more of your problems each quarter is also, if you let it, the thing eroding your ability to judge whether its shaping was right.

Ben Shneiderman's human-centered AI framework gives the corrective its shape. Against the one-dimensional intuition that *more automation means less human control*, Shneiderman argues the two are separate axes: you can have high automation *and* high human control at once [High]. Plot usage level on one axis and supervision intensity on the other, and Priya's failure is legible instantly — she climbed the automation axis through the day while staying pinned to the bottom of the control axis. The diagonal, where supervision rises to meet usage, is the calibrated zone. Below it lies the danger corner Shneiderman warns about: high automation, collapsed control. "Level III work, Level I habits" is the name this book gives that corner.

> **AI Wayback Machine — Douglas Engelbart, "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework" (1962).** Engelbart's SRI report — the project that gave us the mouse and hypertext — argued that tools do not merely make a human faster at a fixed task; they restructure the human–tool system, so that as the tool's share of the work grows, *the human's role changes in kind, not just in degree.* That is precisely the claim under this chapter. Moving from copy editor to supercollaborator is not "more AI." It is a different kind of partnership, demanding a different kind of supervision. Engelbart saw augmentation as having levels sixty years before the supercollaborator existed. What he could not have anticipated is the specific modern hazard: a tool whose role has changed in kind while the human's supervision has not.

## Worked example: Priya's week, mapped

Lay Priya's Tuesday on the two-axis grid and the lesson is exact.

- **9:15 — variable rename.** Usage: Level I. Supervision: a glance, Level I. *On the diagonal.* Calibrated. The four-second glance is not negligence; it is the right tool. Note this carefully, because the lesson is not "scrutinize everything heavily" — that would be its own failure, paralysis dressed as diligence.
- **11:40 — source comparison.** Usage: Level II. Supervision: a glance, Level I. *One step below the diagonal.* The token-lifetime claim needed a check against the vendor docs — thirty seconds of leaving the screen — and did not get it. A wrong figure is now laundered into a design doc, where it carries the authority of the document rather than the uncertainty of an unverified AI claim. (Hold the word *laundered*; it becomes a technical term in Chapter 9.)
- **3:20 — architecture proposal.** Usage: Level III. Supervision: a glance, Level I. *Two steps below the diagonal — the danger corner.* The output reshaped the problem, importing a consistency model whose assumptions are wrong for her regulatory context. This needed supervision of the *framing*: what does this architecture assume about failure tolerance, and are those assumptions true here? That supervision never engaged.

The diagnosis writes itself: Priya's supervision was *flat* across a day in which her usage *climbed.* She was excellent at 9:15 and dangerous at 3:20 with the identical habit, because the habit that fit the first task was a liability by the third. The failure is not in any single decision. It is in the *lag* — supervision calibrated to a lower level than the work, the gap invisible because the interface and the confidence never changed. That invisibility is the whole problem. Nothing announces the floor moving from Level I to Level III. You have to track it yourself. Which is what the inventory below trains you to do.

## Exercises

Graduated; the third produces the baseline artifact you return to in the final chapter. Together they constitute **Supervision Lab Exercise #1 (25 points)** — the gate from Act One into the five capacities.

**Exercise 3.1 — Classify five interactions (Analyze).** Take five AI interactions — your own from this week, or five provided ones — and classify each by level (I, II, III). For each, state the supervisory demand the level imposes and whether your actual supervision met it. *Deliverable:* a five-row table: interaction, level, demand, supervision given, gap (if any). *The judgment this surfaces:* whether you can read the level off the *work* rather than the *tool* — a domain-knowledge call only you can make, since only you know what the output had to be grounded against. (8 pts)

**Exercise 3.2 — Diagnose the mismatch (Evaluate).** Given a provided case of a professional operating at Level III with Level I habits, name the specific failure the mismatch is heading toward — not "an error" but the particular wrong outcome the unsupervised framing produces. *Deliverable:* a short diagnosis naming the failure mode and the moment supervision should have re-engaged. *The judgment this surfaces:* your ability to see a wrong *problem* being solved precisely — which requires knowing what the right problem was, a domain judgment the AI cannot make for the person in the case. (9 pts)

**Exercise 3.3 — Personal AI-usage inventory (Create).** Inventory your own current AI practice. Classify your recurring uses by level, and for each, identify the supervisory gap — where your habitual scrutiny falls below what the level demands. *Deliverable:* a written inventory: recurring use, level, current supervision, gap at this stage. Keep it. It is your Week-1 baseline, returned at the end of the book to document what changed. *The judgment this surfaces:* your accountability for your own practice — naming, before anyone audits you, where you are most likely operating in the danger corner. (8 pts)

## Closing: the diagnosis is complete

Act One is finished. You have seen the failure (five rooms), you have the structural reason it is permanent (the solve-verify asymmetry), and you now have the diagnostic that tells you *when* the demand is highest (the three levels, and the mismatch that produces the danger corner). What you do not yet have is the *content* of level-appropriate supervision — the actual capacities that, exercised, would have caught each failure and closed each gap. Those are the five the book now builds, one at a time. And the first is the one that runs *before* verification even begins: the judgment that decides whether a confident output needs checking at all, and what to check for. We turn to plausibility auditing.
