# Trust, Context, and Accountability

*Distributed intelligence, institutional knowledge, and values that have cost something.*

## A decision defended in a room

A tumor board meets on a Thursday morning. The case is a sixty-one-year-old patient with an aggressive cancer and a complicating heart condition; the question is whether to pursue an intensive treatment that might extend life by months at a real risk of killing her sooner, or a gentler path that concedes the months but protects the time she has. The oncologist has run the patient's profile through a decision-support model. The model returns a recommendation, with a confidence figure and a clean summary of the evidence base.

Nobody in the room treats the recommendation as the decision. The surgeon knows something the model does not: this hospital's ICU is short-staffed this month, which changes the real risk of the intensive path in a way no published survival curve captures. The nurse who has spent the most time with the patient knows that what she actually fears is not death but losing her clarity in her last weeks — a value that reframes the whole trade-off. And someone in that room will sit with the family that afternoon and say, in the first person, *here is what we recommend and here is why* — and will carry the weight of having said it if it goes wrong. The model produced a recommendation. The room produced a decision. Those are not the same thing, and the difference is this chapter.

## The evidence: intelligence that doesn't live in any one head

Three capacities bundle here — distributed intelligence, institutional knowledge, and accountability under pressure — and they're bundled because they share a property: they don't reside in information, so more information doesn't supply them.

Start with the group. Woolley and colleagues (*Science*, 2010) studied 699 people working in small groups and found a **collective intelligence factor** — a "c-factor" — that predicted how well a group performed across very different tasks [Medium-High, and partly contested]. The striking part: this group intelligence was only weakly related to the members' individual IQs (correlations of roughly .15 with average IQ, .19 with the smartest member). What predicted it instead was *social*: the average social sensitivity of the members, how evenly conversational turns were distributed, and the proportion of women in the group. Group intelligence, in other words, ran on relational machinery — sensitivity, turn-taking, the reading of one another — not on the sum of individual smarts.

I have to flag the contest, because the honesty rule requires it and because it's a real one. Later re-analyses (Credé & Howardson, 2017; Bates & Gupta, 2017) argued that the *general* c-factor is weaker than first claimed — that it explains less cross-task variance than general intelligence does for individuals, and may be more task-specific than "a factor" implies. So cite Woolley for the **qualitative point**, which survives the contest — group performance is not reducible to members' individual intelligence, and the drivers are social — and treat the strong "general factor" claim as **suggestive, not settled** [Medium-High for the qualitative point; the general factor is Contested]. The relevance to AI is the qualitative point: real distributed intelligence runs on stakes, trust, and reading the room, none of which an AI participant supplies.

Next, institutional knowledge. Acemoglu (*The Simple Macroeconomics of AI*, 2024) draws a line between **easy-to-learn tasks** — those with objective verification criteria, where AI's productivity gains concentrate — and **hard-to-learn tasks**: context-dependent decisions with *no objective outcome measure* [Medium-High]. The deeper root is Polanyi's paradox (Michael Polanyi, *The Tacit Dimension*, 1966): "we know more than we can tell" [High, foundational]. Much of what an organization knows is *tacit* — acquired through embedded experience, never written down, and therefore in no training corpus. There is no data that captures *this* organization at *this* moment: which coalition will revolt if the reorg goes one way, why the numbers from that one region are always two weeks stale, what the regulator's posture really is this quarter. A model trained on the world's text is frequently *technically correct and practically wrong*, because the binding constraint is the context it cannot have.

It helps to make Acemoglu's distinction operational, because it is the most usable test in this chapter. Put two questions to any task. *Is there an objective criterion that says whether the answer was right?* And *how much does the right answer depend on context a model couldn't have?* A task with a clear right answer and low context-dependence — transcribe this audio, extract these fields, summarize this document — is easy-to-learn, and it is where AI's gains concentrate. A task with no objective outcome measure and heavy context-dependence — should we settle or fight, which coalition will revolt, is this the moment to override the model — is hard-to-learn, and it is the AI-resistant quadrant. The reason that quadrant resists is not that the problem is "harder" in some abstract sense. It is that there is no answer key to train against and no corpus that holds the local facts, so a standardized model is frequently *technically correct and practically wrong*. The binding constraint is context, and context is precisely what a model trained on the generic world cannot have about your specific organization at this specific moment.

Finally, accountability and values. Shannon Vallor (*Moral Deskilling and Upskilling in a New Machine Age*, 2015) argues that automating morally-loaded decisions risks **moral deskilling** — the atrophy of the practical wisdom, the *phronesis*, that comes only from repeatedly *making* hard calls and bearing their cost [Medium]. This is argued philosophically more than measured empirically; hold it as a reasoned position, not a demonstrated finding. But it has one piece of hard empirical support pointing its way, carried over from the calibration chapter: in the automation-bias studies, participants who believed they were **accountable** for the outcome verified the automation more and made fewer errors (Skitka & Mosier) [High]. Accountability is not a feeling. It measurably changes what people do.

## The mechanism: the transcript versus the stakes

Here is the sharp way to see what AI can and cannot do in this territory.

AI can produce the **transcript** of every one of these things. It can generate a meeting in which two positions argue; it can write a memo summarizing this organization's apparent norms; it can compose a fluent ethical rationale weighing the values in the tumor-board case. The *artifact* — the visible output of distributed intelligence, institutional judgment, or moral reasoning — is within reach, and getting more so.

What it cannot produce is the **substance** underneath each artifact, because the substance is constituted by something AI structurally lacks: *stakes* [Medium — mechanism-plausible and philosophically grounded, not empirically tested].

Trace it through each. Distributed intelligence works — when it works — because the people disagreeing have something on the line. The disagreement is productive precisely because the surgeon will own the surgical outcome and the oncologist the medical one; the turn-taking and social sensitivity Woolley found matter *because* they're how accountable people negotiate real risk. An AI can generate the words of disagreement, but it holds no stake, so the disagreement is theater — it cannot be *wrong in a way that costs it anything*, which is the only thing that makes the conflict load-bearing. Institutional judgment is hard-to-learn because the relevant facts are tacit and local and never written down; an AI's summary of "this organization's norms" is reconstructed from the generic, which is exactly where it's technically-correct-and-practically-wrong. And moral agency — the willingness to assume responsibility, to defend the call to the family, to be the name on the decision — cannot be delegated by definition. As the framing goes: *values that have never cost anything are not values.* You discover what you actually hold by paying for it.

The Woolley finding deserves one more turn, because it tells you *what* the AI can't supply, not just *that* it can't. The drivers of group intelligence were social sensitivity, even turn-taking, and a composition effect — not raw IQ. Read that as a claim about *machinery*: a high-functioning group is a machine for distributing attention, surfacing dissent, and reading what people aren't saying, and that machine runs on relational signals that exist only among parties who have stakes and can read each other. Drop an AI participant into that group and it can contribute information and even plausible argument, but it cannot take a turn in the social sense — it has no read on the room, no relationship to preserve or risk, no stake that makes its dissent cost it anything. It can produce the *transcript* of a good meeting. It cannot be a member of one. (Again: hold the general c-factor as contested; what survives the contest is the qualitative claim that the machinery is social, and that is the claim doing the work here.)

There is an atrophy mechanism here, and it's honestly the thinnest-evidenced in the book, so I'll mark it clearly. The claim that **AI-mediated collaboration erodes team dynamics** — that routing collaboration through a chatbot weakens a group's conflict-management and shared alignment — is plausible and analogically supported, but no clean study demonstrates it [Low]. I include it as a hypothesis worth watching, not a finding. The stronger atrophy claim is Vallor's [Medium]: reflexively deferring to the algorithmic recommendation, never making the hard call yourself, lets the moral muscle waste — you keep the *form* of being the decider while outsourcing the *act* of deciding. Her term, *phronesis*, the practical wisdom Aristotle put at the center of good judgment, is explicitly a *trained* capacity — you acquire it the way you acquire any skill, by repeated practice under real stakes. A profession that routes its hard calls through a model is, on Vallor's account, declining to do the reps that build the judgment, and discovering only later that the judgment isn't there when a call falls outside what the model can handle.

## What it means for you

The reframe is this: in this territory, the binding constraint is usually *not* information. It is accountability and tacit context. That's why a better-informed AI doesn't replace the decider — the decider's job was never mainly to be informed. It was to be *responsible*, and to hold context no corpus holds.

Carry the durability claim straight. These are among the *last* capacities AI threatens — they sit furthest from what a next-token predictor does. But "last" is not "never." The worry on the horizon is that agentic systems pipelining whole organizational workflows accumulate enough org-specific context (retrieval over internal documents, persistent memory of how this company works) to erode even the institutional-knowledge advantage. The tacit, never-written-down residue is the durable bet — Polanyi's "more than we can tell" is by definition not in the documents. But this is durable-but-not-permanent, on a long timeline, and I won't pretend otherwise.

There is at least one existence-proof that this boundary is operationally real, not just theoretically tidy. When the fintech Klarna automated a large slice of its customer-service operation and then, by its own later account, moved to rehire humans for "oversight, interpretation, and contextual judgment" — the work that turned out to need a person — it traced, in a single corporate arc, exactly the line this chapter draws between the easy-to-learn artifact and the hard-to-learn call (a named-firm case, so time-stamp it; such cases age fast). The pattern is the tell: automate the transcript, discover you still need the substance, and rehire for the substance. The substance was the accountability and the context all along.

The practical reading: the parts of your work that carry *real accountability* — where you'll defend the decision to a client, a court, a patient, a board, and own it if it's wrong — are the parts least exposed and most valuable. And the trap is laundering those decisions through the tool: "the model recommended it." The moment you do that, you've given away the one thing that wasn't automatable, and — if Vallor is right — you've started the atrophy that makes you worse at it.

## The move: inventory and own your accountable decisions

Take an hour and make a list of the decisions you actually make in your work. Then mark each one.

**First, separate the recommendations from the decisions.** A recommendation is something you produce that someone else owns. A decision is something *you* own — where, if it goes wrong, it's your name and your judgment on the line. Be honest; most people make fewer real decisions than they think and produce more recommendations than they admit.

**Second, for each real decision, mark whether it carries accountability you can't delegate.** The test: is there a room — literal or figurative — where you'd have to *defend* this, in the first person, to someone with a stake? The tumor board, the client meeting, the board presentation, the design review where you say "I made this call and here's why." Those are your accountable decisions. They're the core of what AI-proofs you, and the inventory usually surprises people about which work that actually is — it's rarely the work in the job title.

A useful frame as you sort the list: which of these decisions would survive being summarized into a memo and emailed to someone who'd then decide? Those are the recommendations — the artifact is the deliverable, and AI can help you build it. Which ones require *you* to be in the room, in the first person, reading the faces and bearing the weight? Those are the accountable decisions. The tumor board, the client across the table, the board presentation where you say "this is my recommendation and I'll own the outcome." The tell is that the value isn't in the information — a better-informed memo wouldn't replace you — it's in the accountability and the tacit read of *this* situation that no summary carries. Those are the decisions that AI-proof you, and the inventory routinely surprises people about which work that actually is. It is rarely the work in the job title; it is usually the work the job title was a proxy for.

**Third, commit to owning those visibly — and refusing to launder them.** Use AI freely to *inform* them: summarize the evidence, draft the options, stress-test your reasoning. But when you make the call, make it in your own voice, defend it on your own context and values, and never say "the AI recommended it" as if that transferred the responsibility. It doesn't. The accountability stays with you whether you own it or hide from it — and owning it visibly is both the thing that protects your role and, per Skitka and Mosier, the thing that makes you decide better.

It's worth holding the contrast as a simple two-column ledger, because it clarifies where to stand. On the left, what AI can now produce: the meeting minutes, the policy summary, the recommendation with a confidence figure, the fluent ethical rationale, the draft of the decision memo. On the right, what only a human supplies: the stake that makes disagreement productive [Medium], the tacit institutional context no corpus holds [High, via Polanyi/Autor], the trust that lets a group function as more than the sum of its members [Medium-High, qualitative], and the accountability to defend the call and bear its cost [High, that accountability changes behavior; Medium, that delegating it atrophies the capacity]. The left column is getting longer and cheaper every year. The right column is where your durable value sits — and notice that every item on the right is constituted by something the left column structurally lacks, which is *having something on the line*. That is not a temporary gap in the technology. It is a category difference: a system that cannot be harmed by being wrong cannot supply the thing that only the possibility of harm creates.

There is a deeper lineage worth one sentence, because it broadens who gets to claim this ground. Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel for showing how real communities govern shared resources — not through the textbook's markets or top-down rules, but through trust, monitoring, and graduated accountability built up among people who had to live with each other and with the consequences. That is collective and institutional judgment in its highest form, and it is made of exactly the relational, stake-bearing, locally-tacit material this chapter says AI cannot supply. The capacities here are not exotic. They are the oldest way humans have ever solved hard problems together, and they remain the part of the work that is most yours.

## Bridge

These capacities — supervision, causal reasoning, problem formulation, accountable judgment — are real and durable. But the book has been building toward an uncomfortable point about all of them: they are exactly the capacities that *using AI badly* quietly erodes. The next chapter makes that warning concrete, with the strongest evidence in the book, and starts with a London cab driver's brain.
