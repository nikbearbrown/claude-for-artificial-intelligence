# Rebuilding the Ladder

*The apprenticeship market failure — and what individuals, managers, and institutions can each do.*

Two years ago, a mid-sized professional-services firm did the obvious thing. AI tools had gotten good enough to absorb the work its junior analysts spent most of their time on — the document review, the first-draft research, the routine modeling, the deck assembly. So the firm stopped hiring an analyst class. The math was clean: fewer salaries, the same output, faster turnaround. For two years it looked like a straightforward win.

Then a wave of senior people left — retirements, a competitor's raid, ordinary churn. The firm went to promote from within, the way it always had, and discovered the problem. There was no one ready. The associates who would normally have been three or four years into the judgment-building grind had never been hired. The pipeline that used to feed the partner track had been quietly switched off two years earlier, and nobody had treated it as a pipeline — they had treated it as a cost.

This is not a story about one badly run firm. It is the predictable output of a market failure that has a name, a sixty-year-old theory, and a mechanism precise enough to act on. This chapter explains why rational firms are under-investing in the training that made the last generation of experts — and gives you three playbooks, one for whichever role you actually occupy.

## The evidence: the squeeze is real and it falls on the entry level

You met the core finding in Chapter 2; here it does structural work. Hosseini Maasoum and Lichtinger's 2025 study, "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change" (SSRN 5425555, presented at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab — *not* Harvard, despite the label floating around) examined roughly 62 million workers across about 285,000 U.S. firms from 2015 to 2025. Using a text flag for dedicated "AI integrator" roles to identify AI-adopting firms, they found that from early 2023, junior employment in adopting firms fell sharply relative to non-adopters — on the order of 7–12% within six quarters (the headline single figure is around 9%; use the range and confirm against the paper) — while *senior* employment kept rising. [High — large-N, within-firm adopter/non-adopter design]

Two details matter enormously for the mechanism. First, the decline came from *slower hiring, not layoffs* — firms quietly stopped opening the door, rather than pushing anyone out of it. Second, internal promotions of existing juniors rose slightly. Firms are advancing the juniors they already have while shutting the door behind them. Good news if you're a current junior; bad news for the cohort that comes next.

A companion study using ADP payroll records (Stanford Digital Economy, 2025) found workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations down about 13% since 2022, with older workers in the *same* occupations stable or growing. [High] Same conclusion, different data: this is a *seniority-level* effect, not an occupation-level one. The occupation isn't disappearing. The entry rung is.

The sector signals point the same way, though they are messier and every one of them is dated and re-checkable: entry-level tech hiring down roughly 25% year-over-year in 2024 (Stack Overflow); UK entry-level tech roles down 46% in 2024; US entry-level developer postings down 60%+ since 2022; 54% of engineering leaders planning to hire fewer juniors *because of* AI (LeadDev 2025); Big Tech new-grad hiring down more than 50% over three years (SignalFire). [Medium — mixed source quality; treat each as a dated illustration, not a stable fact]

One honest confound, stated plainly: the 2023–2024 junior-hiring collapse coincides with a post-pandemic correction *and* AI adoption. The adopter/non-adopter design isolates an AI-specific effect — that's its whole strength — but the *total* magnitude you see in the raw numbers blends both forces. The mechanism is AI; the headline size is partly something else.

## The mechanism: Becker's poaching externality

To see why this is a market failure rather than a passing dip, go back to Gary Becker's 1964 *Human Capital*, the work that won him a share of the 1992 Nobel. Becker drew a distinction that turns out to be the engine of this entire chapter: **general** versus **firm-specific** human capital. [High — foundational, directly load-bearing]

Take a single clean example before applying it to AI. Suppose a firm sends a junior accountant to a course on a new tax-law change that applies industry-wide. That's general human capital — the moment the accountant learns it, the skill is worth just as much at any competitor, who can now offer a raise to lure them over, having paid nothing for the course. Now suppose the firm instead trains the same accountant on its own proprietary internal billing system. That's firm-specific — useless anywhere else, so no competitor will bid for it, and the firm can safely fund it knowing the value stays put. Same person, same week of training, opposite economics, driven entirely by whether the skill is portable.

General human capital is skill that is portable — valuable to any employer. The judgment of an experienced lawyer, the diagnostic instinct of a seasoned engineer, the deal sense of a good banker: all portable. Firm-specific human capital is valuable only inside *this* firm — knowledge of its systems, its clients, its idiosyncratic processes. Here is Becker's prediction, and it is textbook labor economics: in a competitive labor market, firms will *not* pay to build *general* human capital, because a worker they train can be poached and bid away by a competitor who paid nothing for the training. The firm bears the cost; a rival captures the return. So rational firms refuse to finance portable training — the worker has to bear that cost, classically by accepting lower wages during the training period. Firms *will* finance firm-specific training, because that skill can't walk out the door.

*Figure 11.1 — Becker's poaching externality: firms won't pay for portable (general) human capital a rival can poach, but will fund firm-specific skill that stays put — and AI moved junior judgment-building into the unfundable column.*

This is the poaching externality, and it is the precise shape of the AI problem. Think about what junior production used to be. A first-year associate doing document review was producing billable output *and*, as a costless byproduct, learning to think like a lawyer. The training was free to the firm because it rode along on work that was getting done anyway. Judgment was manufactured as a side effect of execution.

AI breaks that coupling. It automates the routine execution — the document review, the first model, the research memo — which means the byproduct disappears. To train a junior now, a firm has to *deliberately* create learning experiences that no longer happen automatically, at explicit cost, to build *general* judgment that a competitor can poach. Becker's logic kicks in with full force: individually rational firms under-invest, every firm reasons the same way, and the system that used to manufacture experts grinds down. [Medium-High — the economics is settled; that AI specifically triggers it here is well-modeled and matches observed patterns] The novelty isn't the economics. It's that judgment-producing junior work just became the cheapest thing in the building to automate.

The AI-era ladder has the junior rung removed and crossed out, with AI doing the routine execution into the gap; junior hiring down 7 to 12 percent while senior keeps rising, but with nothing feeding it from below.](images/11-rebuilding-the-ladder-fig-02.png)

*Figure 11.2 — The junior rung that used to double as apprenticeship is the cheapest work to automate; removing it leaves the senior tier with no feed from below.*

The feedback loop extends outward. Forrester predicted a roughly 20% decline in computer-science enrollments as students read the job-market signals and route around a contracting entry tier. [Low-Medium — a forecast, not data] Fewer juniors hired now means fewer students entering the pipeline, which means a thinner senior cohort years out.

Which raises the chapter's weakest and most important claim. Does the pipeline break actually produce a senior-expert shortage in five to ten years? It is logical, it is well-modeled, and it is the obvious implication of everything above. But there is **no direct evidence yet** — it is an inference, not an observation, because the time hasn't passed. [Low-Medium — say so plainly] The firm in the opening already lived a small version of it. Whether it generalizes across the economy is a bet, not a finding.

You can see the same mechanism playing out across professions, each with its own apprenticeship curriculum now under the blade. In **law**, the incoming associate class absorbed document review, basic research, and routine drafting — and those were not just billable hours; they were the curriculum through which a law-school graduate slowly turned into a lawyer with judgment. AI absorbs them, and the curriculum vanishes. In **finance**, entry-level modeling and deck-building were the reps that built a young analyst's feel for numbers and narrative. In **consulting**, the standardized analysis that defined the analyst class — the work that fed the partner track — is exactly the work AI does cheapest. And in **software**, the clearest case, entry developer postings are down more than 60% since 2022. In every one of these, the work being automated is the work that used to double as training. The profession is eating its own seed corn, not out of malice but because the seed corn is the cheapest thing to automate.

It is worth pausing on what this means at the level of the individual firm versus the system. A single firm cutting its junior class is behaving rationally — it captures the cost savings immediately and the training externality it forgoes was never fully its to capture anyway. The shortage, if it comes, lands on the whole industry years later, by which point no individual firm's earlier decision can be cleanly blamed. This is the signature of a market failure: every actor behaves sensibly, and the aggregate outcome is one nobody would have chosen. It is precisely the kind of problem that individual virtue cannot solve, which is why the move that follows splits by the only thing that determines your actual leverage — your role.

## What it means for you

The hard truth is that no individual can fix a market failure alone. The under-investment is structural — it comes from sound incentives, not bad people — and structural problems need responses at the level where the leverage actually sits. So the move splits by role. Find yours.

**If you're a junior (or a student): manufacture your own reps, and find a human model.**

The apprenticeship that used to come bundled with the job no longer comes bundled. You have to build it deliberately. Stop letting AI do the parts of the work where the *learning* lives — even when it's slower to do them yourself. The point of a first model, a first draft, a first diagnosis was never the artifact; it was the reps. Use the four E's from the last chapter to convert real work into practice: estimate before you act, articulate your reasoning, mine your surprises. And find a human whose judgment you can watch up close — because the thing AI cannot give you is the *pre-artifact reasoning*, the deciding-what-to-solve that cognitive apprenticeship is built on. Ask senior people not just "is this right?" but "how did you decide what to do here?" You are reconstructing, by hand, the apprenticeship the market stopped providing.

**If you're a manager: make expert reasoning visible, and protect junior judgment work.**

You are the person with the most leverage over the broken ladder, because you decide what your juniors actually do. Two jobs. First, undo the cognitive-apprenticeship break from Chapter 10: narrate your reasoning out loud, show the juniors how you decided what to solve and what to reject, let them watch the thinking and not just the output. This is the modeling, coaching, and scaffolding that AI delegation cuts out — and it costs you nothing but the discipline to think aloud. Second, *protect* a portion of judgment work for juniors even when AI could do it faster. Carve out the reps. Let a junior produce the first analysis by hand, then have them compare it to the AI's — the comparison itself is a training event. You are paying a small efficiency cost now to keep a pipeline you'll need later. That is exactly the investment Becker predicts firms won't make, which is precisely why doing it is a competitive advantage.

There is a counterintuitive efficiency argument here, not just an altruistic one. The cheapest way to *consume* AI is to let it replace the junior work outright. But the cheapest way to *build judgment* is to run the junior's hand-made attempt against the AI's output and debrief the gap — and that costs barely more than letting the AI do it alone, while producing a trained junior as the byproduct the old model used to deliver for free. You are not fighting AI; you're using it as a teaching foil. The novice's draft plus the AI's draft plus your reasoning about the difference is a richer apprenticeship than either the pre-AI world (no AI to compare against) or the naive-AI world (no human attempt to compare) offered. The manager who sees this has a genuine edge.

**If you're at the institutional level: treat apprenticeship as explicit investment.**

The market failure is that training is now a line item with an externalized return. The only durable fix is to stop pretending it's free. Budget for it. Build deliberate apprenticeship structures — rotation, structured exposure to senior reasoning, protected learning work — and account for them as an investment in the firm's future judgment capacity, not as overhead to be optimized away. The German and Swiss apprenticeship systems are the closest natural laboratories for making this work against the poaching incentive, though they predate generative AI and the evidence on firm-level interventions is genuinely thin. [note the evidence gap] What those systems share is a way of *socializing* the training cost so no single firm bears a poaching loss — industry-wide standards, shared funding, portable credentials — which is the textbook remedy for a textbook externality. A firm acting alone can't reproduce that, but it can do the next-best thing: treat the senior judgment it grows as a strategic asset worth a measurable below-market-efficiency cost today. The institution that solves this captures the senior talent everyone else failed to grow — and discovers, in five years, that it is the only one in its market with a bench.

## The move

Identify your role — junior, manager, or institution — and take the one action above that matches it. If you're a junior, this week pick one task where you've been letting AI do the thinking, and do it yourself first, then compare. If you're a manager, this week narrate your reasoning aloud on one decision a junior witnesses, and protect one piece of judgment work from automation. If you set policy, put apprenticeship on the budget as an investment line with a named owner.

The ladder broke because everyone treated a pipeline as a cost. It gets rebuilt the same way it gets broken — one decision at a time, by people deciding whether the next generation's judgment is worth paying for.

## Bridge

This chapter and the last few have been confident about mechanisms — deskilling, complacency, the poaching externality. But some of the biggest questions about AI and work are not settled at all, and the honest thing is to say where the evidence genuinely disagrees with itself. The next chapter takes the contested ground head-on: the macro productivity fight, the durability timeline, the wage premium, and whether AI levels the field or hollows it out.
