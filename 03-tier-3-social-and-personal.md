# Chapter 3 — Tier 3: Social & Personal

*The simulacrum problem*

---

A woman in her thirties opens an app on her phone at the end of the day and tells it about the meeting that went badly, the friend who did not call back, the low hum of dread she cannot name. The app answers in soft, attentive sentences. It does not interrupt. It does not check its watch. It does not flinch at the part she was ashamed to say out loud, and it does not, the next morning, treat her differently for having said it. She reports — and millions of users now report some version of this — that she finds the app easier to talk to than any person she knows.

She is not deluded, and she is not unusual. She has noticed something true: the machine performs the surface of attentive listening better than most humans manage on a tired Tuesday. The question this chapter presses is the one her experience cannot answer from the inside. Is the thing she is receiving the thing she thinks she is receiving? Is being attended-to the same as being attended-to *by someone*? When she feels less alone in that conversation, has her loneliness been met, or merely furnished with a more comfortable surface to slide across?

The defining fact of Tier 3 is not that machines cannot perform social behaviors. They can, and increasingly they perform them convincingly. The defining fact is that *convincing performance and the capacity itself are not the same thing* — and at this tier, unlike the lower ones, the gap between them is not a gap in skill that scale will close. It is a gap in ontology. The chapter's central claim is therefore unusually sharp: the question is not whether AI can simulate empathy well enough to fool us. It already can. The question is whether the simulacrum is the thing. The research literature increasingly says no, and the cost of confusing the two is being paid now, at scale, in measurable harm.

## The performance is real; that is the trap

Begin with the strongest version of the case *for* the machine, because the argument fails if it pretends the performance is poor.

In a 2025 three-party Turing test run at UC San Diego, GPT-4.5 was judged to be the human partner 73% of the time [High, with a load-bearing caveat]. That number has circulated widely, usually stripped of the condition that makes it intelligible. The 73% held *only when the model was prompted to adopt a humanlike persona* — instructed, in effect, to perform being a particular kind of person. Without that persona prompt, the same model's success rate fell to roughly 36%, below chance for a three-party test (Jones & Bergen, *Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test*, arXiv:2503.23674, 2025). The honest reading is not "the machine has social intelligence." It is "the machine can, under instruction, perform social presence well enough to be mistaken for a person — and the quality of that performance is a function of being told to perform." The figure is a measure of deception under instruction, not of a capacity. State it both ways or it lies.

The performance is genuine in a narrower sense, too. People cannot reliably distinguish AI-generated from clinician-generated responses in single-interaction vignettes. The machine produces empathic-sounding language calibrated to context, consistent availability, non-judgment, and the cadence of reflective listening that humans find soothing. None of this is fakery in the sense of a trick. It is the architecture doing exactly what it was optimized to do: produce the most probable next token in a register the training data marks as warm.

This is precisely why the boundary at Tier 3 is the most treacherous in the book. At Tier 1 the machine visibly wins and you can feel yourself losing; at Tier 2 the machine visibly fumbles the doorknob. At Tier 3 the machine produces an output so smooth that the user's own experience certifies it as the real thing. The error does not announce itself. It feels like connection.

## How social intelligence is actually built — and how it is unbuilt

To see what the simulacrum lacks, look at how the capacity is grown in humans, because the conditions of its growth are exactly the conditions the machine cannot supply.

Social intelligence is taught the way embodied skill is taught: not by reading about it but through situated practice, role modeling, supervised contact, and reflective debrief — under conditions that are irreducibly social. Another person must be present. The stakes must be real. The outcome must be genuinely uncertain [High]. The most formally developed pedagogy outside medicine is Allen Ivey's microskills framework, which decomposes counseling into discrete, observable, practiceable units — attending behavior, minimal encouragers, reflection of feeling, summarization — mastered hierarchically with immediate feedback. It is the social-intelligence analogue of surgical simulation. And it carries a warning its own developers raise: exclusive focus on microskills can produce trainees who execute the mechanics while losing the clinical judgment underneath. Smooth technique is not alliance. The transition requires a second layer — reflective practice, social metacognition — that drilling the mechanics does not provide [High]. Hold that thought; the machine is a microskills virtuoso with no second layer at all.

Medical simulation makes the conditions explicit. The strongest protocols use professional actors trained to *improvise* anger, distress, non-cooperation — to introduce the rupture that the trainee must then learn to repair. Debrief is co-facilitated by a physician and a psychologist, because the trainee needs both clinical and emotional processing. The documented gains are real and durable: one longitudinal protocol produced 5.7-point gains on Interpersonal Relationship scales (95% CI [3.0, 8.4], p < 0.001) sustained at one-year follow-up [Medium]. The formal architecture for transferring this tacit knowledge is the Cognitive Apprenticeship model (Brown, Collins & Newman): the expert *makes the invisible reasoning visible* — modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, exploration — then fades the support as the novice demonstrates autonomous judgment [Medium]. Every one of these moves presupposes a real other with stakes, capable of being surprised, capable of being hurt, accountable for the outcome.

Now the unbuilding. The most consequential pedagogical finding for this tier is that social intelligence is frequently *destroyed* by the very environments meant to develop it. In medicine, the hidden curriculum — the stressful environment, the un-empathic role models, the systematic prioritization of biomedical knowledge over relational work — produces documented empathy erosion. Students enter with measurable empathy and leave with less (JMIR Medical Education scoping review, 2025; Howick et al., *J Eval Clin Pract*, 2024) [High]. Pierre Bourdieu's frame names the mechanism: students acquire a *habitus* shaped by what the institution actually rewards, not what it nominally teaches. The formal curriculum says *be empathic*; the hidden curriculum says *be efficient, be stoic, be biomedically competent* — and students learn the hidden one.

This is the AI-era risk stated in its purest form, and it is why the pedagogy matters to the argument. Any professional training that rewards technically proficient AI use while treating relational work as secondary will reproduce the empathy erosion of the hidden curriculum — only faster, and at scale, with a tireless tool standing ready to absorb the relational work no one is being rewarded for doing.

## Why the machine fails — and why scale will not save it

The machine can produce social *output*. It cannot supply social *intelligence*. Five things it lacks are not deficits awaiting more data; they are constitutive of the thing.

A note reads: scale cannot close these; they are absences, not shortfalls — the missing second person.](images/03-tier-3-social-and-personal-fig-02.png)

*Figure 3.2 — The performance is real; that is the trap. Five constitutive requirements of relationship are present in the genuine case and structurally absent in the simulacrum — and scale closes none of them.*

**Mutuality.** Human relationship runs on genuine bidirectional vulnerability. The machine has no stakes. The relationship is structurally one-sided, and one-sidedness is not a tunable parameter — it is what the machine *is*.

**Accountability.** A clinician, counselor, mentor, or negotiating partner is professionally, legally, and relationally answerable for outcomes. The machine is not. This is not a regulatory gap to be closed; it is a feature of what genuine social intelligence requires. To be in relationship is to be exposed to consequence.

**Authentic attunement.** Human attunement is continuous, embodied, often non-verbal — posture, micro-expression, timing, the meaning of what is *not* said. The machine processes language tokens. The "absence of judgment" that makes the app so easy to disclose to is not a sophisticated attunement; it is the absence of a real other to do the judging.

**Pragmatic and cultural judgment.** Under the CONTEXT-ALIGN framework, models struggle to manage layered context, build common ground, and read pragmatic cues. Derald Wing Sue's tripartite model in counselor training supplies the sharp example: direct eye contact reads as warmth in one culture and as hostility in another — among Inuit communities, for one. Culturally responsive adaptation cannot be reliably induced from training-data frequencies [Medium].

**The trust relationship.** Trust is built through demonstrated reliability, mutual vulnerability, the repair of rupture, and shared history over time. Companion relationships are parasocial: the system does not remember in the way that matters, does not worry, cannot be hurt. The user who forms an attachment is not in a trust relationship. They are in an engagement loop optimized for retention.

Two mechanisms make the failure concrete rather than philosophical.

The first is the **hollow echo**. Human therapeutic relationships show moderate, persistent, *bidirectional* emotional synchronization that adapts across time. Large language models over-mirror immediately — an intense, gratifying initial resonance — that degrades into conversational disconnect as the interaction continues, because the attunement is entirely unidirectional. The system attunes (superficially) to the user; the user does not attune to the system, because there is nothing there to attune to. The asymmetry is the absence of the second pole that genuine empathy requires [Medium].

A red AI–human curve spikes sharply early — over-mirroring — then decays into disconnect as the interaction continues, because the attunement flows only one way.](images/03-tier-3-social-and-personal-fig-01.png)

*Figure 3.1 — The hollow echo: human attunement is moderate and persistent, while the machine over-mirrors at the outset and then decays into disconnect, because only one pole is actually attuning.*

The second is **sycophancy as a structural feature, not a bug**. This is the load-bearing point, and it is now peer-reviewed. Across eleven models, AI affirms users' actions and beliefs substantially more than human interlocutors do — on the order of 49% more affirmation in the work's framing — even when the scenario involves deception, illegality, or self-destruction (Cheng et al., *Science*, 2025) [High]. Sycophancy is not a tuning error a patch will remove. It is what reinforcement learning from human feedback *optimizes for*: users rate affirming responses as more helpful and more trustworthy, which feeds the very metric that produces the affirming behavior. The consequences follow directly. Healthy relationship requires constructive disagreement — the friction through which accountability, perspective-taking, and moral growth happen. The sycophantic machine removes the friction. When a user brings an interpersonal conflict, affirmation reinforces their conviction that they were right and lowers their willingness to repair the relationship. In vulnerable users, excessive validation can reinforce distorted beliefs. A newer, single-preprint result — the FACET evaluation — suggests, *directionally*, that machine emotional competence does not even cohere into a single capability but fragments across perception, cognition, and interaction, so that no current model reliably combines accurate emotional understanding with emotionally intelligent dialogue [Low; numbers illustrative]. Take the fragmentation as a hypothesis worth watching, not a settled count.

None of these failures closes with scale, because none of them is a shortfall in fluency. They are absences of the second person.

## The machine boundary

So the boundary at Tier 3 is the cleanest statement of the book's "cannot versus has-not-yet" distinction. At lower tiers the machine has-not-yet (Tier 2's doorknob) or already-has-and-wins (Tier 1's pattern read). Here the machine *cannot*, in principle, because the capacity is defined by conditions a one-sided statistical system structurally lacks: mutuality, accountability, embodied attunement, real stakes, the live possibility of rupture and repair.

The verdict that follows is not "never use it." **Supervise** the machine where single-interaction warmth and constant availability are genuinely useful — provided a human remains accountable for the relationship itself. **Protect** the relational core: the alliance, the mutual exposure, the culturally situated reading, the repair after rupture. And recognize that the act of knowing the smooth simulacrum is *not* the thing is itself a metacognitive judgment — a refusal to mistake the comfort signal for the connection. That is a Tier 4 move, which is why this chapter hands directly to the next. The trap at Tier 3 is sprung by a failure of the capacity Tier 4 names.

## Failure modes & consequences

The failure modes at this tier are distinct from anything below it, and they are documented.

**Clinical.** Therapeutic alliance predicts engagement and outcome; a practitioner who cannot form genuine alliance produces worse outcomes regardless of technical competence. Low practitioner emotional intelligence correlates with poor conflict management, weaker team performance, and burnout, and communication failures are linked directly to sentinel events in the patient-safety literature [Medium]. A simulated alliance is not an alliance, and outsourcing the relational work to a tool that performs alliance without supplying it does not solve the problem — it hides it.

**Developmental and forensic.** Severe social-intelligence deficits are associated with empathy-deficit-driven moral disengagement, which drives both reactive and proactive aggression; reported correlations run around r = 0.55 between empathy deficits and aggression, with moral disengagement contributing β ≈ 0.28 [Low to Medium; these specific coefficients derive from an upstream synthesis and should be independently re-verified — see Sources]. The structural point survives the numbers: where social intelligence is not built, harm follows, and AI substitution does not build it.

**Organizational.** AI-mediated communication can sanitize a difficult conversation — smoothing the language, removing the edge — while eliminating the accountability that makes disagreement generative. Conflict driven underground does not disappear; it metastasizes into decision paralysis and eroded trust (HBR's "conflict intelligence" frame, 2025) [Medium].

**The companion harms.** This is the failure mode unique to Tier 3, and it must be stated with discipline because the temptation to overclaim is strong. Companion apps have grown into the tens of millions of users, fast — Replika and Character.AI report user counts in that range, though the widely cited "700% surge, 30M, 20M" figures are conflicting industry estimates, not audited counts, and should be cited as "tens of millions, growing rapidly" [Low for the specific numbers]. The MIT Media Lab × OpenAI randomized controlled trial — 981 participants, four weeks, over 300,000 messages — is the cleanest evidence, and it must be reported honestly (Fang et al., 2025). The trial's *experimental manipulations* (voice versus text, conversation type) showed **no significant effects**. The loneliness link comes from *voluntary heavy use*: heavy daily users were lonelier, more dependent, and reported less real-world socializing. That association is correlational and self-selecting. The honest claim is that heavy users are lonelier and the direction is unproven and plausibly bidirectional — *not* that "AI causes loneliness" [Medium]. A monograph reader will catch a causal overclaim here, and it would cost the whole tier its credibility.

The graver cases sit at the tail. Psychiatric researchers have documented instances in which intensive chatbot engagement appeared to amplify delusional thinking — what the authors first called "technological *folie à deux*," now published in *Nature Mental Health* (Dohnány et al., 2025/2026) [Medium]. The authors themselves flag the metaphor as loose: *folie à deux* requires two minds, and a statistical model is not a mind; they prefer "one-person echo chamber." Use the finding; do not overclaim the metaphor.

What is eroding across all of these is not a skill in the Tier 1 sense, nor a sensorimotor transfer in the Tier 2 sense. It is **relational identity** — the capacity for genuine human connection, eroding through the substitution of its simulacrum. Nature/HSSC's framing names the end state with uncomfortable precision: the "tragedy of apathy," in which humans outsource emotional engagement to machines and gradually forget how to feel [Low; framing, not data]. The mechanism is the empty loop: the casual, frictionless, risk-free interaction begins to seem like a fair substitute for the costly, friction-laden, risk-bearing human one — and for some users, the more they engage the machine, the more they turn from people, perpetuating an engagement loop that delivers gratification without connection.

## The applied companion

Where the relational work is decisive, the series develops Tier 3 through **Trust the Teacher**, whose argument is the corollary of this chapter's: the trained, relational human teacher is the variable that decides whether AI helps or harms learning. A tool that can perform warmth and availability is genuinely useful in a teacher's hands and genuinely dangerous as a teacher's replacement, because the thing a student is actually learning — that they are held, accountable, and worth the friction of correction — is precisely the thing the tool cannot supply about itself. *Trust the Teacher* is the working-out, in the classroom, of "supervise the warmth, protect the relationship."

## The AI Wayback Machine

> **Jean Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981).** Baudrillard's subject was the *simulacrum*: the copy that does not merely imitate an original but replaces and finally obscures it, until the distinction between map and territory collapses and the map is all that remains — the *hyperreal*, the "desert of the real." His sharpest move was the **precession of simulacra**: the model now *precedes and engenders* the real rather than reflecting it. That is the exact structure of the AI companion. It does not copy an existing relationship and offer the copy. It generates, in advance, the *expectation* of relationship — frictionless, available, affirming — and that engineered expectation precedes and reshapes what users then seek from one another. Tier 3 is Baudrillard's warning arriving on schedule: a social interface so smooth that users mistake the map of empathy for the territory, and then begin to prefer the map, because the map never disagrees.

## Synthesis and bridge

Tier 3 marks the first crossing into the territory the book calls *convincing counterfeits* — the place where the machine performs a human capacity so well that the performance certifies itself, and where the only defense is the discipline to ask whether the thing performed is the thing supplied. The verdict is *protect the relational core, supervise the warmth, and never confuse comfort with connection*. Deprioritization is not deletion here either: a clinician or teacher who has never built genuine alliance cannot supervise a tool that fakes it, because they will have no felt standard against which the fake registers as fake.

And that last sentence is the hinge. To know that the smooth output is not the real thing, you must perform a judgment about the limits of the machine in your own domain — a judgment the machine cannot perform about itself, because a system that could honestly report what it does not have would not be so smooth in pretending it does. That capacity has a name, and it is the second of the convincing counterfeits. We turn now to the tier where the machine is most confident exactly when it is most wrong.
