## Preface

The question in the title is not rhetorical.

Intelligence is a word that gets used with enormous confidence by people who disagree completely about what it means. Researchers have measured it, mapped it onto brain structure, traced its heritability, and built machines they claim possess it — sometimes in the same breath they claim the machines do not. The disagreement is not minor. It runs to the foundations.

This book follows intelligence backward and sideways: back to organisms without neurons, sideways to creatures whose inner lives we can barely imagine from the outside. A nematode with 302 neurons. A slime mold with no neurons at all. Bees that appear, under some definitions, to have emotional states. Crows that plan for futures they cannot yet see. Language models that ace standardized tests and fail at tasks a three-year-old handles without effort.

The comparison is the point. By holding all of these against the same question — what is intelligence, and does this organism have it? — we are forced to be precise about what we are actually asking. And precision, it turns out, is where the interesting disagreements live.

This is not a comprehensive survey. It is a path through contested territory, argued from a position. I have chosen a working definition, named its limits, and tried to use it honestly throughout. Where the evidence is thin, I have said so. Where the field is genuinely unsettled, I have not pretended otherwise.

The worm in the petri dish is one millimeter long and has been studied more completely than almost any other animal on Earth. By the time you reach the last chapter, I hope you will find that remarkable — and I hope the word *remarkable* will mean something slightly different to you than it did when you began.
