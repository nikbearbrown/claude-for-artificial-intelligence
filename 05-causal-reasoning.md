# Causal Reasoning

*The machine sounds causal. It is not performing causation.*

## The outage that blamed the wrong thing

At 2:14 on a Tuesday afternoon a payments service starts timing out. Latency climbs, error rates spike, the on-call engineer opens the incident channel. They paste the symptoms into an AI assistant and ask for a root-cause analysis. The answer comes back fast and well-organized: latency began rising shortly after a database connection-pool change deployed that morning; the connection-pool change is "the most likely root cause"; recommended remediation is to roll it back.

They roll it back. The latency does not recover. It gets slightly worse, because the connection-pool change had in fact been *compensating* for the real problem — a configuration change two deploys earlier had quietly doubled the number of retry attempts on a downstream call, and the connection-pool tuning was an unrelated, sensible adjustment that happened to ship close in time. The AI had identified the change most *correlated* with the onset of symptoms and named it the cause. It read the timeline and told a story about the timeline. What it never did — what it cannot do — was reason about which change could have *produced* the others, which were symptoms, and what would have happened under a different intervention.

This is the failure this chapter is about. The machine produces causal-sounding language. It does not perform causal reasoning. And the gap between those two things is one of the most durable places human judgment still lives — for now.

## The evidence: fluent about cause, near-chance at finding it

Start with the framework that makes the claim precise. Judea Pearl's **Ladder of Causation** (*The Book of Why*, 2018) sorts all reasoning about cause into three rungs [High]. **Rung 1, association** — *seeing*: what is the probability of Y given that I observe X? This is correlation, pattern, the world as it presents itself in data. **Rung 2, intervention** — *doing*: what happens to Y if I actively *change* X, written P(Y | do(X))? **Rung 3, counterfactual** — *imagining*: what would have happened to Y had X been different, in a specific case that already occurred?

A red dashed theorem barrier sits between Rung 1 and Rung 2, labeled "cannot climb on observational data alone," with the LLM pinned at Rung 1.](images/05-causal-reasoning-fig-01.png)

*Figure 5.1 — Pearl's three rungs and the theorem barrier between association and intervention; a text-trained model can describe the upper rungs but lives on the bottom one.*

The decisive mathematical fact is that **you cannot climb from Rung 1 to Rungs 2 and 3 using observational data alone.** No amount of "X and Y move together" tells you whether changing X changes Y, because the same correlation is produced by three different structures: X causes Y, Y causes X, or some third thing Z causes both. The data are identical in all three cases. To choose among them you need a causal model — assumptions about structure that the data cannot supply. This is not a limitation of current technique. It is a theorem.

A language model trained on text is, by construction, a Rung-1 engine. It learns the statistical structure of language — what words and ideas tend to follow what — which is association all the way down. It can *describe* Rungs 2 and 3 fluently, because human text is full of causal talk, but describing the do-operator is not applying it. And the benchmarks bear this out. On **Corr2Cause** (Jin et al., 2023), a task that hands a model a set of correlations and asks it to name the causal relationship, GPT-4 scored an F1 of roughly 29, with most models near or below chance, and performance collapsed further under paraphrase [High]. On **CLadder** (Jin et al., NeurIPS 2023), a benchmark built directly on Pearl's three rungs, vanilla prompting did poorly; a hand-built, externally supplied chain-of-thought procedure ("CausalCoT") lifted GPT-4 by about eight points to around 70% [High]. Read that result the right way: the model needed a *causal procedure supplied from outside* to approximate causal reasoning. It does not do it natively.

Two honesty notes. First, these benchmark numbers are perishable — they describe a fast-moving frontier and should be re-checked against current models before you bet anything on the specific figures; a 2024 critical review (arXiv:2407.08029) also warns that some causal benchmarks are gameable by memorization, so don't over-read any single score in either direction [Medium]. Second, and more important for the book's honesty rule: there are two different claims here at two different strengths.

The **empirical** claim — *current LLMs fail causal-identification tasks where the answer isn't memorizable* — is well-supported on present models [Medium-High, edging to High on current systems]. The **theoretical** claim is stronger and more contestable. It comes from Felin and Holweg, "Theory Is All You Need" (*Strategy Science*, 2024) — and note the attribution, because an earlier synthesis of this material wrongly credited it to "Gurerk et al." [Medium]. Felin and Holweg argue that human cognition is *theory-based*: forward-looking, able to hold a belief that runs *ahead of or against* the available data, and from that belief to design an experiment that *generates new data*. Their example is heavier-than-air flight — the data said "birds, not machines"; the theory said otherwise, and the theory was right. AI, on their account, is *data-based*: backward-looking, imitative, bounded by the distribution it was trained on. Imitation, they argue, can never *originate* a causal theory, because origination means going beyond the data, and going beyond the data is the one thing a system that predicts from the data structurally cannot do.

That is a strong, elegant argument. It is also an *argument*, not a settled fact — scaffolding, tool use, and agentic pipelines are improving causal benchmark scores, and reasonable researchers disagree about whether causal competence can eventually be engineered around the base model. Hold the empirical claim firmly and the in-principle claim as a well-reasoned position. Do not launder the second into the first.

It is worth seeing the same failure across fields, because the surface differs and the structure is constant. In **medicine**, an AI attributes an outcome to a treatment when the real driver is a confounder — sicker patients selected into the treatment, so the treated group looks worse and the model reads "treatment harms." In **finance**, a model reads two assets co-moving because of a common shock as a hedging relationship, and someone builds a position on the spurious link. In **marketing**, a sales lift that was seasonal gets attributed to the campaign that happened to run during the season. In **operations**, the human has to *specify* the intervention and rule out the alternatives; the AI can summarize an A/B result but cannot decide what comparison would actually identify the effect. Each is a different costume on one body: the model found an association and dressed it as a cause, because association is the only thing it was built to find, and causal language is the only thing it was built to say about it.

## The mechanism: why "it explained why" fools you

Here is why the outage engineer was fooled, and why you will be too if you let the prose decide.

When you ask an AI *why* something happened, it gives you an explanation that is locally coherent: each sentence follows plausibly from the last, the vocabulary is causal ("led to," "triggered," "as a result of"), and the chosen cause is genuinely correlated with the effect. All of that is real. None of it is identification. *Identification* is the work of deciding which variables matter, recognizing the confounder that produces a spurious link, orienting the arrows (does the config change cause the latency, or do both trace to a third event?), and ruling out the alternative stories that fit the same evidence. The model performs none of these steps. It retrieves and assembles the *language* that surrounds causal reasoning in its training data.

The trap is that this often produces the right answer — which feels like vindication and is actually the problem. The AI is frequently right about causes *because the answer was already in the text it learned from*: the common causes of common problems are written down everywhere. The corr2cause result is the tell, because there the answer is *not* memorizable — you have to actually infer structure from correlation — and there the model drops to chance. So "the AI is usually right about causes" and "the AI reasons causally" are different claims, and the gap between them is exactly where it will hand you a confident, fluent, wrong root cause for the novel problem — which is the only kind of problem worth paying a human to diagnose.

And now the link to the previous chapter's hardest problem: auditing an AI's causal story requires running *your own* causal model. To catch the misattributed root cause, you have to independently ask which variables, which confounders, which alternative explanations, which arrow directions. If you have outsourced that work to the tool, you have nothing to audit it with. Which sets up the atrophy risk that the deskilling chapter will make concrete: delegate diagnosis long enough and you stop building the causal models that let you supervise the diagnosis. The capacity erodes precisely as you lean on the thing that was supposed to let you lean on it.

This is not a new worry; it is an old discipline. Long before Pearl formalized the do-operator, the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill (1965) gave practitioners a checklist for inferring causation from association — strength of the relationship, consistency across settings, the right temporal order (cause before effect), a dose-response gradient, plausibility of a mechanism, and the experimental test where one is possible. The Bradford Hill criteria are, in effect, the practitioner's pre-formal version of exactly the audit this chapter teaches: a set of questions you run *against* a tempting correlation before you call it a cause. The reason they survive is that they encode the move from Rung 1 to Rungs 2 and 3 in plain professional language — and they make vivid that this move was always *human work*, done by people who had to be right about whether smoking caused cancer when the data alone could not settle it. The AI gives you a fluent narrative; Bradford Hill gives you the interrogation that narrative has to survive.

It helps to hold a single picture of the whole problem. Imagine a scatter plot: two variables that clearly move together, a tight diagonal cloud of points. That picture is Rung 1, and it is genuinely all the data contains. Now imagine three small diagrams beside it, each a different arrangement of arrows that would produce *that exact same cloud*: X causing Y, Y causing X, and a hidden Z causing both. The data cannot distinguish them — the cloud is identical in all three worlds. The only thing that picks the right diagram is a causal model: assumptions, knowledge of mechanism, an intervention you could run. The AI sees the cloud and narrates one of the three arrangements, usually the most familiar-sounding one, with complete confidence and no way to know it chose. *You* are the part of the system that can look at the cloud, hold up the three candidate diagrams, and do the work of ruling two of them out. That work is the durable contribution. The cloud is free; the diagram is yours.

## What it means for you

The durable human contribution in causal work is **identification, not narration.** The AI will narrate; that part is solved and getting cheaper. What it cannot reliably do — and what your value increasingly consists of — is the upstream judgment about structure: which factors to include, which to suspect of being confounds, what the counterfactual world looks like, which rival explanation has to be killed before the favored one survives.

Carry the durability claim honestly, though. This is durable **on the current frontier and on a timeline**, not forever. The benchmark gaps are closing; agentic systems with explicit causal scaffolding do better than raw models; the broader projection that most text tasks reach high automation by the end of the decade applies here too. So the right frame is not "causation is permanently human." It is: *the identification step is yours for now, and the only way to keep it yours is to keep doing it.* The capacity that survives is the one you exercise.

One more honesty note, because the comparison can be read as flattery of the human and it shouldn't be. The claim is not that people reason causally well and machines don't. People commit causal fallacies constantly — we see faces in clouds and agency in coincidence, we confuse the salient with the causal, we are exquisitely vulnerable to a good story. The claim is narrower and conditional: *the audit requires human causal reasoning, and the human is at least capable of it,* whereas the model is structurally not performing it at all. A skilled human can climb the ladder; an unskilled one often won't bother. Which is exactly why the capacity is durable only if trained — and why the move below matters more, not less, in a world where the tool will always offer to skip it for you.

## The move: the alternative-explanation drill

Take one real decision you're about to make on AI input — a diagnosis, an attribution, a root cause, a "this caused that" claim driving an action. Before you accept the AI's causal story, run three steps. It takes ten minutes and it forces the Rung-2/3 work the model skipped.

**One: draw the arrows.** Sketch the variables as a quick diagram — boxes for the things involved, arrows for "this causes that." You don't need formal notation; you need to commit to a *structure*. The act of drawing forces you to decide what causes what, which is the question the AI silently skipped.

**Two: name two confounders and one reversed arrow.** For the proposed cause-and-effect, ask: what third thing could produce *both*? (The seasonality behind the sales lift; the sicker patients who selected into the treatment; the common shock moving two assets together.) And ask: could the arrow run the other way — is the named "cause" actually a symptom? Write down two candidate confounders and one plausible reversal, even if you end up dismissing them. Most misattributions die at this step.

**Three: state the counterfactual and how you'd test it.** Finish the sentence "if the proposed cause were *not* present, I'd expect to see ___" — and then "the cheapest thing that would distinguish my story from the alternatives is ___." Often that cheapest test is a five-minute check (look at last quarter, pull the deploy two hops back, segment the data). Run it.

If the AI's story survives all three, trust it more — you've now actually identified, not just read. If it doesn't survive, you've caught the thing the fluency was hiding. Either way you've spent ten minutes keeping a capacity alive that the tool would otherwise quietly take.

A word on why the drawing matters, since it's the step people skip. The diagram you sketch is, in miniature, what Sewall Wright invented in the 1920s as *path analysis* — the direct ancestor of the modern causal graph, and the formal object Pearl later equipped with the do-calculus. The reason these arrows have been worth a century of work is that they make a *commitment* the prose never forces: a sentence can hedge ("the change may have contributed to the latency"), but an arrow cannot. You either draw it or you don't, and you either draw it pointing this way or that. That forced commitment is the whole value — it converts a vague causal feeling into a structure you can interrogate, and interrogating structure is the work the model declined to do. You don't need Wright's mathematics or Pearl's calculus to get the benefit. You need the discipline of committing to arrows, because the commitment is where the thinking lives.

And keep the time-box honest. The point of ten minutes is not that ten minutes makes you a causal-inference expert; it's that ten minutes is enough to catch the *gross* errors — the reversed arrow, the obvious confounder, the seasonal effect dressed as a treatment effect — which is most of what goes wrong. The drill is calibrated to the threat. The AI rarely fails on the textbook case where the answer was in its training data; it fails on the novel case, and the novel case is usually one where a single confounder or a single reversed arrow is the whole story. Ten minutes of structure-drawing is sized exactly to that failure mode.

## Bridge

Causal reasoning lets you audit the answer to a question. But a deeper kind of judgment runs *upstream* of any question — the work of deciding which problem is even worth solving. No prompt resolves an underspecified brief, because the gap isn't in the answer; it's in the question. That is the next chapter.
