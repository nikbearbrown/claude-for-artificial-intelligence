# Chapter 5 — The Assumption Underneath the Assumption

> *Why fixing errors within the model is easier than questioning whether the model itself is wrong — and why defensive reasoning is the primary obstacle to discovering what you don't know you don't know.*

---

## Learning objectives

1. *(Understand)* State the difference between **single-loop** and **double-loop** learning in plain language, and identify what kind of unknown unknown each can and cannot reach.
2. *(Analyze)* Climb a sample reasoning sequence up Argyris's **ladder of inference** and identify the rung where the unwarranted move occurred.
3. *(Apply)* Diagnose a real disagreement (your own or in a public record) as a gap between **espoused theory** and **theory-in-use**.
4. *(Evaluate)* Decide whether a given organizational response to a failure is single-loop or double-loop — and defend the call.

## Opening case — Columbia, foam, and the conversation NASA could not have

On February 1, 2003, the Space Shuttle Columbia disintegrated during atmospheric re-entry. Seven crew members were killed. The proximate cause: a piece of insulating foam, dislodged from the external tank 82 seconds after launch, struck the leading edge of the left wing, breaching the heat shield. The breach was not repairable in orbit; on re-entry, hot gas entered the wing and the orbiter broke apart.

The *Columbia Accident Investigation Board* (2003) traced a deeper cause. Foam shedding had been observed on prior flights — many prior flights. Each observation was classified as an "in-family event," a recognized phenomenon within the operational envelope, and the launch program continued. Engineers had argued, in some cases vigorously, that the framing was wrong — that foam shedding was not within the qualified envelope, that the absence of catastrophic failure on past flights did not establish safety, that the system was operating in a regime no one had qualified it to operate in.

What the report names, in the language of organizational theory: NASA's launch culture was running on **single-loop learning** — adjusting actions within a fixed model of "what we expect, what we tolerate, what we treat as routine." The deeper question — *is the model itself wrong?* — required **double-loop learning** and was systematically deflected by what Chris Argyris called *defensive reasoning*.

This chapter is about the conversation that did not happen at NASA and the structural reasons it did not happen. The unknown unknown was not the foam. The foam was a known unknown that had been re-classified as a known known. The unknown unknown was the *frame inside which the foam's significance was being interpreted*. That frame is what double-loop learning addresses.

## The core concept — two loops

Imagine a thermostat. The thermostat's job is to keep the room at 70°F. It senses the temperature; if the room is below 70°, it turns on the heat; if above, it turns the heat off. This is **single-loop** behavior: the thermostat acts within a fixed goal and adjusts its actions to meet the goal.

Now imagine the thermostat could ask a different question: *is 70°F the right goal?* Maybe the room's occupants are warmer than typical and 68° would be better. Maybe the heating cost has changed and the goal should shift. Maybe the room is being used for a different activity than the original setting accounted for. Asking *is the goal right?* is **double-loop** behavior: the system inspects its own governing values and revises them.

Chris Argyris's contribution (with Donald Schön; primary texts *Theory in Practice*, 1974; *Organizational Learning*, 1978; *Organizational Learning II*, 1996) is to apply this distinction to organizations and individuals. Most organizational learning, Argyris argued, is single-loop. The organization detects an error; the organization adjusts within the existing strategy; the strategy itself remains unchallenged. Double-loop learning — examining and revising the governing values that produced the strategy — is rarer because it is *threatening*: it requires the people who hold the values to admit the values were wrong.

A first technical term: **the ladder of inference**. Argyris (the version in *Overcoming Organizational Defenses*, 1990) describes how individuals move from observable data to action through a chain of cognitive steps — selecting data, assigning meanings, making assumptions, drawing conclusions, adopting beliefs, taking action. Most steps happen automatically and without awareness. The ladder is the mechanism by which two people looking at the same data arrive at different conclusions: they selected different observations, assigned different meanings, made different assumptions.

A second term: **espoused theory vs. theory-in-use**. *Espoused theory* is what a person or organization *says* governs their behavior — the values stated in mission statements, performance reviews, internal communications. *Theory-in-use* is what *actually* governs their behavior, inferred from the pattern of decisions. The two are often different and the gap is mostly invisible to the person whose decisions reveal it. NASA's espoused theory included "engineering concerns trump schedule pressure"; the theory-in-use, the Board found, did not.

A third term: **defensive reasoning**. The cognitive moves that protect a person from facing a threatening conclusion. Defensive reasoning is rational in a narrow sense — it preserves the person's view of themselves as competent — but it makes double-loop learning impossible. Argyris's empirical work documented these moves in dozens of organizational settings. The pattern is consistent across contexts.

## Worked example — the engineers' email and the management response

The Columbia investigation surfaced specific email exchanges between engineers and managers during the flight. Engineers raised the possibility that the foam strike had caused damage that could be fatal on re-entry. Managers, citing the in-family classification and the absence of repair options, declined to request imagery from the Department of Defense satellites that could have characterized the damage.

The exchange is not a story about bad people. It is a story about the operation of defensive reasoning at scale. The engineers' espoused theory: raise concerns when concerns exist. The engineers' theory-in-use, the report found, included a strong norm against escalating concerns that managers had already classified as resolved. The managers' espoused theory: take engineering concerns seriously. The managers' theory-in-use included a strong norm against re-opening classifications when doing so would imply that the existing classification was wrong.

Both sides were operating within their theories-in-use. The gap from the espoused theories was real, structurally produced, and invisible to most of the participants. Defensive reasoning is what kept the gap invisible: each participant could justify their individual moves; the system-level pattern those moves produced was not anyone's responsibility to see.

What double-loop learning would have required: a member of the organization willing to name the gap between espoused theory and theory-in-use in public, in a way that did not produce defensive reasoning in the people whose behavior was being described. Argyris's empirical claim, with which the chapter agrees: this is much harder than it sounds. Most attempts to surface the gap trigger the defensive reasoning the gap is the symptom of.

**The lesson:** The unknown unknown at NASA was not a missing technical fact. It was the structural pattern by which a known concern was kept from the people who needed to act on it. That pattern is invisible to single-loop learning because single-loop learning operates within the frame the pattern produces.

**The limit:** Even Argyris's empirical work documents successful double-loop interventions as rare and skill-intensive. The chapter does not promise a recipe. It teaches the diagnosis. The fix, when it works, is slower and more painful than the diagnosis suggests.

## Common misconceptions

**"More open communication solves this."**
Argyris's specific empirical claim is that requests for "more open communication" usually produce more *espoused* openness without changing the *theory-in-use*. The defensive reasoning is structural; verbal exhortation does not reach it.

**"This is a leadership problem."**
The defensive-reasoning pattern is documented at every level of organizations Argyris studied. Leadership behavior amplifies or attenuates it but does not produce or eliminate it. The pattern lives in the implicit norms about what conversations are safe to have in the organization, which are produced by many actors over time.

**"Psychological safety solves it."**
The psychological-safety literature (Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*, 2018) is a successor to Argyris's work and addresses some of the same patterns, but it operates primarily on the willingness-to-speak axis. Argyris's framework operates on the *willingness-to-be-changed-by-what-is-spoken* axis. The two are different and complementary.

**"Double-loop is just questioning assumptions."**
Questioning assumptions is necessary but not sufficient. Double-loop learning specifically requires *changing* the governing values when the questioning reveals them to be inadequate — which is the hard part. Most assumption-questioning is followed by defensive reasoning that re-installs the original frame slightly modified.

## Exercises

1. *(Analyze)* Take a recent disagreement where you and a colleague reached different conclusions from the same data. Climb the ladder of inference on each side: what data was selected? What meanings were assigned? What assumptions were made? Identify the rung at which the paths diverged. Notice how rapidly the cognitive moves happened.

2. *(Apply)* Identify one piece of feedback you received in the last year that you dismissed. Examine the dismissal: was it single-loop (the feedback didn't fit within your existing model and you corrected the surface fact) or double-loop (the feedback challenged the model itself and you considered revising it)? If single-loop, articulate what the double-loop version of the response would have looked like. Be honest about what would have made it harder.

3. *(Evaluate)* Take an organization you participate in (work, volunteer, community). Identify one espoused theory it states publicly. Identify the theory-in-use you can infer from its pattern of decisions. State the gap in one sentence that names the divergence without attributing it to bad actors. Test the sentence: would the people whose behavior produces the gap recognize it as a fair description?

## What would change my mind

If empirical work demonstrated that organizations achieve durable double-loop learning at meaningful rates through standard interventions (training programs, communication restructuring, leadership coaching), the chapter's claim that double-loop learning is hard and rare would weaken. The available evidence (Argyris's own studies; Edmondson; the broader organizational-learning literature) is more consistent with the hard-and-rare claim than against it. New large-scale evidence of reliable double-loop interventions would shift the chapter's emphasis toward practice and away from diagnosis.

## Still puzzling

- The boundary between productive single-loop refinement and ducked double-loop revision is not crisp. Many improvements are legitimately within-frame; the question is when within-frame fixes stop being adequate and the frame itself is the problem. Argyris's framework does not give a clean threshold.
- The defensive-reasoning analysis is most rigorous on individual cognition. Scaling it to organizational systems requires connecting individual moves to system patterns, which Argyris does but which other organizational-learning frameworks (Senge, Chapter 9) handle differently.
- The chapter's main empirical case (Columbia) is post-hoc. The harder question — whether double-loop learning can be induced *before* the failure — remains under-evidenced. Chapter 6's practices section addresses some attempted answers.
