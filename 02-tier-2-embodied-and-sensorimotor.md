# Chapter 2 — Tier 2: Embodied & Sensorimotor

*Moravec's paradox; the boundary is physical.*

In a robotics lab in 2026, a humanoid that can pass a graduate physics exam and write competent code is asked to do something a toddler does without thinking: open an ordinary door. The handle is a lever, not the round knob it was tuned on. The door is slightly ajar, which it did not expect. The threshold has a lip. The machine hesitates, recalculates, reaches, mistimes, and fails — not catastrophically, just ordinarily, the way a thing that does not have a body in a world fails. The same week, in a teaching hospital across town, a surgical resident learns to set a suture not from a manual or a video but from an attending's hand resting lightly on her wrist, transmitting through skin and bone the exact force at which tissue yields before it tears. No words pass that could carry the information. The knowledge is in the hand.

Hold those two scenes side by side, because the gap between them is the subject of this chapter — and the gap runs exactly opposite to our intuitions about what is hard. We assume the physics exam is the impressive thing and opening the door is trivial. The machine reveals the truth: the exam is easy and the door is nearly impossible. This inversion has a name, and it is older than the current AI moment.

A caution before we begin, because this is the chapter most exposed to the moving boundary: treat the door as a dated illustration, not a permanent ceiling. By the time you read this, some lab may have a robot that opens that particular door. The structural point survives anyway, and learning to tell the structural claim from the capability snapshot is itself a skill this chapter means to teach.

## Moravec's paradox, still holding

In 1988, the roboticist Hans Moravec stated the inversion that would carry his name. In *Mind Children,* he wrote: "It is comparatively easy to make computers exhibit adult-level performance on intelligence tests or playing checkers, and difficult or impossible to give them the skills of a one-year-old when it comes to perception and mobility." [High] His explanation was evolutionary. The sensorimotor skills humans perform effortlessly — grasping, balancing, walking over uneven ground, manipulating an object we have never seen before — are the product of many millions of years of selection, refined into machinery so deep and so optimized that it runs below awareness. Abstract reasoning, by contrast, is an evolutionary newcomer, a thin recent layer not yet compressed into anything efficient. So the things that *feel* hard to us, because we have to attend to them, are computationally shallow; the things that feel effortless are computationally vast. We mistake the felt difficulty for the real one.

Nearly four decades on, the paradox still holds. A 2026 review in *Nature Machine Intelligence* restates it flatly — "Moravec's paradox still holds" — observing that "cognitive processes such as abstract reasoning, which humans regard as demanding, are often easier for machines than the sensorimotor skills that humans can perform effortlessly," and reaching for the same illustration this chapter opened with: commercial robots still struggle to open ordinary doors in all their real-world variation. [High] And the roboticist Rodney Brooks, who has spent a career building physical machines and watching the hype cycle from the inside, predicts that deployable humanoid dexterity will remain "pathetic" compared to human hands well beyond 2036 — calling much of the current humanoid-dexterity marketing "pure fantasy thinking" and naming *tactile sensing* as the missing ingredient that learning-from-video cannot supply. [Medium] This is the defining structural fact for Tier 2: the machine boundary is not only cognitive. It is physical.

It is worth meeting the obvious objection head-on, because it is the one a well-informed reader will raise before the end of this paragraph: *but robots are getting so good.* They are — and the chapter concedes it loudly rather than pretending otherwise. Investment in humanoids is enormous, bipedal locomotion has improved dramatically, warehouse manipulation of known objects is a solved-enough problem to deploy commercially. The concession is not a weakness in the argument; it is the setup for it. The advances cluster in *structured* environments — known geometries, controlled lighting, surfaces that stay where they were yesterday. The frontier that resists is the *unstructured* world: the construction site that is different every hour, the kitchen drawer that sticks, the patient whose anatomy does not match the textbook. Epoch AI's 2026 analysis identifies construction precisely as a domain where autonomous robotics faces structural rather than incidental barriers — sites change constantly, surfaces are unpredictable, and the work demands adaptive physical judgment in real time. [Medium] So the right reading of "robots are getting so good" is not that the boundary is dissolving but that it is *relocating* — retreating from the structured tasks, where the machine now wins, toward the unstructured and integrative ones, where the human body still has no peer.

## Why the hand is so hard

Consider what the surgeon's hand actually is. It is commonly modeled as having 27 degrees of freedom — the precise count depends on convention; 22 and 26 also appear depending on how the wrist and certain joints are tallied, so the careful phrasing is "commonly modeled as 27 degrees of freedom." [High] It carries thousands of tactile receptors and is governed by one of the largest territories of the motor cortex devoted to any body part. Robotic hands have made real engineering progress — the literature traces genuine advances in joint transmission systems across two decades — but every current design embodies a deep trade-off. Gear trains give you stiffness and torque but introduce backlash that corrupts force feedback. Tension-drive systems give you compliance but suffer cable stretch and wear. At the *system* level — where perception, actuation, control, and durability must all work together, continuously, in an environment that will not hold still — robots remain far from human versatility. [Medium]

Three algorithmic bottlenecks compound the hardware problem, and one of them illustrates exactly why this chapter must distinguish a structural limit from a temporary one.

The first is *latency.* Human motor control runs through spinal reflex arcs of roughly 10 milliseconds — fast enough to catch a slipping glass before the conscious mind registers the slip. [High] A large monolithic Visual-Language-Action model, by contrast, can take up to 500 milliseconds to compute an action policy from its billions of parameters, which is far too slow for closed-loop physical control. [Medium] Here is the temptation: to read "500 milliseconds" as a permanent wall. It is not. The field's 2026 answer is *dual-system architectures* that split the timescales — NVIDIA's GR00T N1 pairs a slow vision-language planner with a fast diffusion control policy; Figure's Helix runs a slow vision-language model at 7–9 Hz behind a fast reactive visuomotor policy at 200 Hz. [Medium] The naive single-model approach is too slow; the workaround is hierarchical, and it works. So latency is *not* the durable barrier. What does not go away when you split the timescales is the *integration* problem — getting perception, fast control, slow planning, tactile feedback, and physical durability to cohere into reliable behavior in an unstructured world. That is the structural claim, and it survives every advance in raw speed.

The second bottleneck is *spatial hallucination.* When a language model errs, it produces wrong text, recoverable and cheap. When a robotic arm errs by a millimeter, it crushes the object it meant to grasp. [Medium] The physical world does not forgive small errors the way a text buffer does, which raises the reliability bar to a height that text-domain success never had to clear.

The third is *action-data starvation.* The internet is saturated with text and images and starved of the thing physical manipulation models actually need: high-fidelity action data — joint torques, haptic force streams, the coordinate transformations of a hand closing on a real object. [Medium] You cannot scrape touch. And this points at the deepest limit of all: proprioception and interoception, the body's continuous sense of its own position and internal state, are architecturally distinct from anything in current AI. Human bodies integrate feedback from muscles, joints, skin, and organs through dedicated neural pathways that are not analogous to a sensor array bolted onto a chassis. [Medium] Whether this gap closes with more data or is fundamentally architectural is an open scientific question — Brooks and the embodied-cognition camp say architectural (you cannot learn touch from video); scaling optimists disagree. This book sides with the structural view, and presents it as a reasoned position in a live debate, not as settled fact.

## How embodied skill is actually taught

The pedagogy of the body has known for a long time what AI is now rediscovering: this knowledge cannot be transmitted as text. The dominant mode is apprenticeship, and the research on tacit knowledge transfer is remarkably consistent across surgery, the skilled trades, and athletic coaching. Novices accept demanding conditions — long hours, repetition, close supervision — primarily to be *near* expert practitioners, because the knowledge being transferred is not in any text. It is in the doing, and in the proximity to someone doing it well. [High]

Underneath apprenticeship sits a deeper tension that motor pedagogy has mapped carefully: the gap between *explicit* learning (rule-based, conscious control of the body) and *implicit* learning (the non-conscious acquisition of movement patterns). Explicit instruction helps a novice grasp a basic sequence, but it produces fragile motor schemas that fall apart under stress. Implicitly learned skills are far more resilient when the environment is fast and uncertain. [Medium] Elite youth athletes with superior implicit-learning capacity reach peak motor performance earlier than their peers — suggesting that the capacity for implicit sensorimotor acquisition is itself a trainable, developmentally significant form of intelligence. [Medium] To drive implicit adaptation, coaches deliberately use the *contextual interference effect:* forcing unpredictable task-switching so the learner cannot consciously fixate on mechanics. Contemporary frameworks — ecological dynamics, nonlinear pedagogy — go further still, treating skill not as a fixed pattern to install but as something that *emerges* from the continuous interaction of performer, task, and environment, shaped by manipulating constraints rather than prescribing an ideal form.

The domains specialize this in instructive ways. Surgical training runs modular simulations from basic instrument handling through force-control on variable-resistance materials to bimanual suturing, governed by the Dreyfus skill model (novice → advanced beginner → competent → proficient → expert) and increasingly by *mastery learning* — practice to a defined standard, not to a fixed number of hours. [Medium] Dance and physical therapy use kinesthetic light-touch cueing to direct attention to internal support without triggering the cognitive interference that verbose verbal instruction would cause — implicit learning, applied. In the skilled trades the picture is starker: there is no pedagogical infrastructure comparable to medical simulation, only apprenticeship, governed by what the economics literature frankly calls an *incomplete contract* — an agreement to transfer knowledge that cannot be fully specified in advance, because if it could be specified it would not be tacit. [Medium]

## What AI can add — and the line it cannot cross

None of this means technology is useless to the teaching of embodied skill. It is genuinely useful — as an *assistant,* not a substitute for the doing. VR simulation allows unlimited repetition without patient risk and is validated for basic surgical skills. Motion capture and video analysis surface performance feedback that unaided expert observation misses. Dual-user haptic devices can transmit an expert's tool trajectory directly to a novice's hand in real time — the closest the field has come to digitizing tacit physical transfer. fNIRS neural monitoring can measure cognitive workload during training and adjust difficulty to the learner's actual state. [Medium] AI is genuinely excellent at making invisible patterns visible.

But there is a line, and it is best drawn as a table that this chapter treats as its signature artifact — the gap between what motion capture can *digitize* and what it cannot:

| AI can help digitize | Harder to digitize |
|---|---|
| Joint angles | Felt effort |
| Motion trajectories | Confidence and hesitation |
| Error frequency | *Why* the error happened |
| Timing | Rhythm, flow, intention |
| Force estimates | Tissue feel, tool feel, material feel |
| Video patterns | Contextual judgment |
| Performance metrics | Professional identity and responsibility |

Right, "stays in the body": felt effort, confidence and hesitation, why the error happened, rhythm and intention, tissue feel, contextual judgment, identity and responsibility.](images/02-tier-2-embodied-and-sensorimotor-fig-02.png)

*Figure 2.2 — Motion capture digitizes the measurable shadow of a skill; the skill itself — felt effort, why an error happened, the read of tissue — stays on the far side of the fault line, in the body that earned it.*

The left column is the measurable shadow of a skill; the right column is the skill. And the gap is not merely a matter of better sensors. Kinesthetic feedback in most VR simulators remains absent or inadequate — commercial haptic devices typically render relatively low stiffness (on the order of a few newtons per millimeter), while cortical bone demands far higher force reflection, and maintaining a stable haptic loop requires end-to-end latency below roughly 1–5 milliseconds, beyond which the trainee feels a disconnection and force spikes that would damage real tissue. [Medium] The deepest failure mode is what the literature calls *sensory fragmentation:* a simulator that feeds only the visual channel forces the brain to encode a motor memory missing its somatic, kinesthetic, and proprioceptive components. When the trainee then meets real tissue, those expectations are simply absent from the motor program — and performance collapses at precisely the moment it matters most. More immersion does not automatically yield more transfer; a 2024 study of haptic feedback in 360-degree VR found increased presence and engagement but no significant difference in decision-making performance. [Medium]

This is what *deprioritization is not deletion* looks like in its embodied form: you cannot digitize what you have not yourself felt. The expert is required not just to perform the skill but to know what the motion-capture data *means* — which deviations are errors and which are a master's adaptation, what the force curve felt like from the inside. Hand the measurement to the machine; keep the meaning in the body that earned it.

## The invisibility norm and the Cartesian inversion

So far this chapter has argued that the machine cannot do this. The second, more pointed argument is that our curricula do not teach it — and not by oversight but by inheritance.

The pattern is what the research calls an *invisibility norm:* physical, sensory, and manual capabilities are assumed self-evident and left largely unscaffolded in higher education, treated as things one simply picks up rather than as forms of intelligence to be developed. [Medium] The root is Cartesian dualism. Our academic models systematically privilege disembodied, propositional cognition — "knowing that" — while marginalizing procedural, craft-based expertise — "knowing how." Analytic skill is "academic"; embodied skill is "vocational," which in the unspoken hierarchy means lesser. The consequence is a *knowledge drain:* when experienced practitioners retire, they take with them tacit expertise that no curricular architecture was ever built to capture, leaving a workforce that can describe physical systems it can no longer expertly manage.

The embodied-cognition literature suggests the hierarchy is exactly backward. Conceptual thinking, it argues, unavoidably depends on perceptual and sensorimotor engagement — body-based learning is not a lesser way of knowing but a *precondition* for deeper cognitive development. [Medium] And the AI era hands this argument a sharp new edge. The capacities our curricula treat as supplementary are precisely the ones whose human distinctiveness is now *most* defensible — the ones the machine cannot reach. The relationship to the machine at Tier 2 therefore *flips* relative to Tier 1. At Tier 1 the danger was over-competing with a superior machine. Here the machine does not win, and the danger is the opposite: *under-valuing* a human capacity because the curriculum never named it as intelligence in the first place.

This inversion is not a rhetorical flourish; it changes what a curriculum should measure. Recall the implicit/explicit distinction from the pedagogy of the body. The skills that survive stress and transfer to novel situations are the *implicitly* acquired ones — the ones that cannot be fully verbalized, that live below the level of rules, that an examiner cannot capture in a written test. These are exactly the skills our assessment regimes are worst at seeing, because our assessment regimes were built on the Cartesian assumption that what matters can be stated. The result is a systematic mismatch: we measure the explicit, propositional layer (which the machine can now also produce) and neglect the implicit, embodied layer (which the machine cannot). A curriculum that took embodied intelligence seriously would invert this — it would treat the felt force of tissue yielding, the dancer's sense of where her weight is, the welder's read of the puddle, as the core competency to be developed and assessed, and the recitable facts as the easily-replaced periphery. That inversion is what the Tier 2 argument demands, and it is the precise opposite of what most programs currently do.

> **The machine boundary at Tier 2.** Bounded — not effortlessly, not reliably, not in unstructured settings, not at human system-level integration. Machines advance fast in *structured* domains (warehouse pick-and-place with known geometries, manufacturing, bipedal locomotion on known surfaces) and remain durably limited where the environment will not hold still. The robust, dating-resistant claims are three: fine dexterous manipulation in unstructured environments; system-level integration; and tactile sensing and proprioception as architecturally distinct from current sensor arrays. The latency numbers and the door example are 2026 snapshots; the integration and tactile gaps are the structure. Brooks's "pathetic beyond 2036" is a forecast about the structure, not the snapshot.

> **Failure modes & consequences.** *Knowledge drain* — tacit expertise lost at retirement with no architecture to capture it, because the curriculum never treated it as knowledge worth capturing. *Sensory fragmentation* — visual-only simulators that build motor memories missing their somatic component, so that performance degrades catastrophically at the transition to the real, high-stakes environment. Both follow from the same root error: mistaking the digitizable shadow of a skill for the skill.

## Bridge to Tier 3

The applied companion to this tier is *Embodied Teaching,* which draws the protected-core boundary in the concrete settings where it matters — the woodshop, the operating room, the nursing simulation lab, the physical-therapy clinic, the music studio, the dance floor — and works out, domain by domain, what to digitize and what to keep in the body.

Note the recursion once more, in its Tier 2 form. To distinguish a *structural* limit (touch, proprioception, system integration) from a *temporary* one (this year's door) is not itself a sensorimotor act. It is a metacognitive, and ultimately a causal, judgment about *why* a limit holds — exactly the higher-tier capacity the rest of the ladder will develop. The reader who can make that distinction is already practicing the audit at the level the book most wants.

Tier 2 widened the boundary from thinking to *being a body in a world,* and that widening is the hinge into everything above it. If machines struggle with physical embodiment — with having a body that feels effort and consequence — then we should expect them to struggle, in deeper and less visible ways, with the capacities that *depend* on having such a body: genuine relationship, real stakes, the felt weight of a decision. The next rung is where the machine stops failing visibly, the way the door defeats it, and starts succeeding deceptively — producing a flawless social performance with nothing behind it. Where the embodied gap was obvious, the social gap will be a counterfeit good enough to fool us, which makes it the more dangerous.

> **AI Wayback Machine — Hans Moravec, *Mind Children* (1988).** "It is comparatively easy to make computers exhibit adult-level performance on intelligence tests or playing checkers, and difficult or impossible to give them the skills of a one-year-old when it comes to perception and mobility." This is the rare Wayback figure who is not a distant ancestor of the chapter's idea but its living center: Tier 2 *is* Moravec's paradox, turned from an observation about robots into a principle for a curriculum. The capacities a one-year-old masters are the ones our schools have never thought to teach as intelligence — and the ones a machine, decades on, still cannot reach.
