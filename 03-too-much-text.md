Here is a thing I want you to understand before we go any further: "too much text" is not a diagnosis. It is the observation you make when you have not yet done the diagnosis. And if you treat it as a diagnosis — if you reach for the delete key the moment a slide feels heavy — you will fix the wrong thing almost every time.

## The machinery underneath

Your working memory has two channels. Not one — two. There is a verbal channel, the one that processes language. There is a visual-spatial channel, the one that processes images, diagrams, and spatial relationships. The important thing is that these two channels are largely independent. A diagram and a spoken explanation can run simultaneously without degrading each other, because they are drawing on different cognitive resources. That is the core design fact, and it is well-established: Richard Mayer and his colleagues ran the relevant experiments over several decades, and the architecture is robust.

Now here is the problem. Text on a screen is processed through the verbal channel. Your voice is processed through the verbal channel. When I stand in front of a class and speak while a paragraph is visible on the slide behind me, I have put two language streams into the same limited pipeline. They compete. One wins; the other is suppressed. Usually the screen wins, because the screen is stable and effortless to attend to — it demands nothing. I become the audio track playing while someone reads.

![Dual-channel working memory diagram ](images/03-too-much-text-fig-02.png)
*Figure 3.2 — Dual-channel working memory diagram *

That is the underlying machinery. But here is the thing: this explains only one of three distinct ways a slide can fail. If you know only this one failure mode, you will correctly diagnose about one-third of your broken slides and misdiagnose the other two-thirds. So let me take the three apart separately, because they have different mechanisms, different cures, and different evidence behind them.

---

## Three failures, not one

**The first failure is redundancy.** The text on the slide says what you are about to say out loud. The verbal channel receives the same content twice — once from your mouth, once from the screen — and the collision degrades both. The fix that comes to mind is usually "cut the text." But that is the wrong operation. The explanation belongs in the notes field, where it serves the student who studies from the exported deck later in silence. It does not belong on the slide body, where it competes with your voice during the lecture. Mayer calls this the Redundancy Principle, established experimentally in 2008: students given animation plus narration outperformed students given the same animation, the same narration, *and* on-screen text that duplicated the narration. The extra text did not help. It actively made learning worse.

**The second failure is seductive detail.** The text is interesting, but it is not essential to what the student needs to learn from this slide. A date. A name. A biographical aside. A piece of historical color that arrived in the deck because it arrived in the source material and nobody asked whether it belonged. The mechanism here is different from redundancy — this is not about channel collision. This is about schema competition. The student's brain builds mental models around whatever information is most prominent and emotionally salient. An interesting anecdote is more salient than a structural claim. So the student remembers the anecdote and integrates the structural claim poorly. Sandra Harp and Richard Mayer ran six experiments on this in 1998, and the result was consistent: lessons with seductive details — interesting but tangential material — produced roughly one-third the retention of structurally important content compared to stripped versions. The effect is strongest when the anecdote appears early in instruction, when prior knowledge is low, and when the core content is already demanding. Which is to say: the first slide of an introductory lecture on a difficult topic is the worst possible place for a fascinating aside.

The fix here is not to move the text. It is to delete it from the slide body — and if it has study value, to bury it at the end of the notes field under "Background," where a curious student can find it after the structural content is already encoded.

**The third failure is density.** The text is all essential — no redundancy, no seductive detail — but it arrives as a single working-memory load. A sentence carrying four structural facts. A paragraph that requires two readings before it resolves. The mechanism here is cognitive overload: the student saturates before the content is encoded, not because the content is wrong but because the presentation requires too much simultaneous processing. John Sweller's cognitive load theory, and Mayer's Segmenting Principle derived from it, point to the same thing: identical content sequenced into smaller labeled chunks produces better learning than the same content presented as a wall. Working memory handles one chunk at a time. Present three at once, and it handles none of them well.

The fix here is neither deletion nor migration. It is segmentation — breaking the dense sentence into labeled pieces, or splitting the slide into two or three slides that reveal the content in sequence.

Three failures. Three different operations. Notice what they do not share: the thing you cut for Type 1 is the thing you should move, not delete. The thing you cut for Type 2 is genuinely removed, though not necessarily thrown away. The thing you "fix" in Type 3 is not cut at all — it is restructured. "Less text" as an instruction conflates all three operations into one, and that is why the fix so often produces a slide that is sparser but equally broken.

## What a broken slide looks like

Let me make this concrete. Here is a slide produced by an AI tool given the instruction "make a slide on the subprime mortgage crisis." The title is "The Subprime Mortgage Crisis and Its Causes." Below the title, 119 words of body text, formatted as a single justified paragraph at 18pt:

![Mockup of the slideument slide ](images/03-too-much-text-fig-03.png)
*Figure 3.3 — Mockup of the slideument slide *

> The subprime mortgage crisis emerged in the United States between 2007 and 2010, triggered by a sharp decline in home prices that followed nearly a decade of rising house values. Lenders had expanded mortgage origination to borrowers with weaker credit profiles — so-called *subprime* borrowers — often through adjustable-rate mortgages whose initial low payments reset to substantially higher payments after two to three years. When the housing market peaked in 2006 and began to decline, these resets coincided with declining home equity, producing widespread defaults. Lehman Brothers, founded in 1850 by Bavarian immigrants and one of Wall Street's oldest investment banks, filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008, an event widely regarded as a turning point in the crisis.

Start at the top. The title is "The Subprime Mortgage Crisis and Its Causes." That is a filing label, not a claim. It tells the student which region of economic history this slide inhabits. It does not tell them what to think about it. A claim — the thing a headline should actually be — would read something like "Subprime ARM resets met falling home equity: defaults followed." That is a sentence with a subject and a predicate. The student who walks away from that headline has an assertion to test their understanding against. The student who walks away from "The Subprime Mortgage Crisis and Its Causes" has a tab in a folder.

Now work sentence by sentence through the body.

The first sentence — "The subprime mortgage crisis emerged in the United States between 2007 and 2010..." — is exactly what the speaker is about to say aloud. This is a Type 1 failure: redundancy. Verbal channel collision begins the moment the slide goes up and the speaker opens their mouth.

The second sentence — "Lenders had expanded mortgage origination to borrowers with weaker credit profiles..." — contains four structural facts compressed into a single sentence: what a subprime borrower is, what an adjustable-rate mortgage is, the reset window, and the magnitude of the payment change. This is a Type 3 failure: density. Each of those four facts deserves a moment of processing. Delivered as a single sentence while the speaker is already talking, none of them gets that moment.

The last sentence — "Lehman Brothers, founded in 1850 by Bavarian immigrants..." — is a Type 2 failure: seductive detail. The 1850 date and the Bavarian immigrants clause are genuinely interesting. They are also irrelevant to the structural argument the lecture is building. By Harp and Mayer's analysis, that clause is not neutral decoration — it is actively competing for schema-building attention. The student who walks away remembering that Lehman Brothers was founded by Bavarian immigrants in 1850 is the student whose brain encoded the migration story instead of the mechanism of a financial crisis. The interesting detail did its job too well.

Three failures, in 134 words, on a single slide. All of them invisible under the label "too much text."

## What the repair looks like

Now here is the same content, with different decisions applied.

![Mockup of the repaired slide ](images/03-too-much-text-fig-04.png)
*Figure 3.4 — Mockup of the repaired slide *

The headline becomes the claim: *Subprime ARM resets met falling home equity — defaults followed.* Nine words. A full sentence with a subject and a predicate. The student who reads only the headline leaves with a structural assertion they can test.

The body becomes three labeled chunks:

- **Borrowers:** weaker credit, adjustable-rate mortgages.
- **Trigger:** ARM resets coincided with 2006 price peak.
- **Result:** widespread defaults, 2007–2010.

Each chunk is a working-memory-sized unit. One fact. A label so the student knows what category it belongs to. The speaker can explain each chunk while the student processes it — slide and narration working in sequence rather than colliding.

The notes field carries the prose. Every sentence that was going to be delivered verbally moves there. The Lehman date and the Bavarian immigrants clause move to the end of the notes under "Background" — recoverable for the curious student studying from the export, absent from the live deck where they would otherwise capture the room's attention. Nothing is thrown away. Everything is in the file. It is just in the right part of the file.

Total words visible on the live slide: 36. Not 134.

Now notice what made the repair possible. The headline made it possible. Without a specific claim, there was no basis for running the three-failure-mode audit. The Bavarian immigrants clause had no center of gravity to be measured against. "Is this essential?" is an unanswerable question on a slide with no stated claim. "Does this support the assertion that ARM resets met falling home equity and defaults followed?" is an answerable question, and the answer is no.

This is the non-obvious thing about headlines. A headline does not just improve a slide aesthetically. It makes the slide auditable. A topic label cannot be audited by failure type, because the failure types require a learning objective to measure elements against, and a topic label is not a learning objective.

---

## Where AI tools fail here, and why

The AI tool asked to "make a slide on the subprime mortgage crisis" produces 134 words because 134 words is what a finished treatment of the topic looks like in its training data. The model is optimizing for apparent completeness — for the thing that looks done at midnight when the slide-builder is tired. The student's working memory is not in the model's loss function.

The same problem arrives when the prompt is "too much text — cut it." The model has no way to know which text to keep, because deciding which text to keep is the prior diagnostic work. With an under-specified instruction, the model strips words indiscriminately. The result is a slide that is sparser but equally broken — Type 2 detail still present, Type 3 density still unstructured, Type 1 prose still in the body where it competes with the speaker's voice — just in fewer words. The instruction "less text" is not wrong. It is incomplete. It collapses three different operations into one and asks the model to guess which operation applies. The model guesses by surface appearance. Surface appearance is not the right criterion.

The prompt that changes this makes the three failure modes explicit:

---

*Audit this slide for text density using the three-failure-mode test. For each text element on the slide:*

*1. Is this text repeating, word-for-word or in paraphrase, what the speaker will say aloud? If yes — TYPE 1 (Redundancy). Move it to the notes field.*

*2. Is this text interesting but not essential to a stated learning objective for this slide? If yes — TYPE 2 (Seductive detail). Remove it from the slide. If it has any retention value, move it to the end of the notes field under "Background."*

*3. Is this text essential but presented as a dense paragraph or complex multi-fact sentence? If yes — TYPE 3 (Density). Segment it into short bullets of eight words or fewer, organized by category labels, or split the slide into two or three slides revealing the content in sequence.*

*After auditing, rewrite the slide with: one headline as a full-sentence assertion of twelve words or fewer; at most six bullets of eight words or fewer supporting the headline; no body paragraph; the full reading text in the notes field. List in a comment which failure mode applied to each cut.*

---

The audit step is the teaching. A naive "reduce text" prompt cuts indiscriminately. The three-failure-mode audit produces cuts you can defend, and relocations rather than losses — so the notes field ends up carrying what was always meant to be there, and the slide body carries only what the student needs to see while the speaker talks.

## The three principles, named

Redundancy, Coherence, Segmenting — these are three separate Mayer principles, all arriving at the same broken slide.

The Redundancy Principle (Mayer & Johnson, 2008) is behind Type 1. The Coherence Principle, anchored by Harp and Mayer's seductive-details experiments (1998), is behind Type 2. The Segmenting Principle, drawn from Sweller's cognitive load theory, is behind Type 3.

![Three-node diagram mapping failure types to their research](images/03-too-much-text-fig-05.png)
*Figure 3.5 — Three-node diagram mapping failure types to their research*

What is striking about this is that the three principles are almost never applied together, because the vocabulary for doing so is not how they usually appear in the literature. They appear in separate papers, under separate headings, tested in separate experimental contexts. A faculty member who has read Mayer knows about redundancy. The same faculty member may not have connected it to Harp and Mayer's seductive-details work, which is in the same tradition but less cited in practical guides. Sweller's work is often treated as a separate literature entirely.

What I am giving you in this chapter — three failure modes as a unified diagnostic — is a synthesis, not a direct citation from any one paper. The evidence behind each principle is strong. Whether diagnosing the type before cutting produces measurably better outcomes than a simple word-count reduction is, to my reading, not yet directly tested. The claim I hold with confidence is the direction: treating three different mechanisms as one produces one-size-fits-all cures that work on the most common case and miss the other two. The faculty member who knows only to "reduce text" will consistently fail the slide whose text is essential but dense — because the fix for density is segmentation, not deletion, and those are different operations.

## A reasonable disagreement

Two faculty members can agree a slide has too much text and disagree honestly about where to stop cutting. The disagreement worth naming sits between two real schools of thought.

One school, represented by Garr Reynolds' *Presentation Zen*, pushes toward the extreme: slides that approach a single image and a short phrase, or sometimes a single word. The speaker carries the full explanation. The slide is closer to a magazine cover than a textbook page. The argument is that the Modality Principle supports this — narration plus visual dominates text plus visual for novice learners, and a slide that competes with the speaker for the verbal channel is always losing ground. On this view, my 36-word repaired slide is still too long.

The other school, represented by Michael Alley's assertion-evidence framework and Cliff Atkinson's *Beyond Bullet Points*, prescribes a different limit: a headline that asserts a claim, supported by sparse evidence. The resulting slide runs higher word count than Reynolds — 20 to 40 words is typical — but in technical disciplines where the claim itself requires precision, that structure arguably does real cognitive work. The student who can read "Subprime ARM resets met falling home equity" while the speaker elaborates knows which argument is being constructed. The student staring at a single evocative photograph may not.

Both approaches are better than the bullet-only topic-label default. Both share the diagnostic this chapter teaches. Where they disagree is the upper bound on visible text, and the resolution depends on the discipline, the audience's prior knowledge, and the speaker's ability to carry the full structural argument without the slide holding it.

The question that cuts through the disagreement: *is the headline a label or a claim, and how much evidence does the audience need to see on the slide while the speaker explains it?* Reynolds answers "minimal — I am the evidence." Alley and Atkinson answer "enough that the student can follow the structural argument in real time." Both are defensible. You will land somewhere between them, and the chapter is content with that. The point is not the word count. The point is that whatever text you leave on the slide was audited — type by type — and the cuts have reasons.

## LLM Exercise

Take a slide from a deck you have already built — one you suspect has too much text — and paste it into a language model with this prompt:

*Run the three-failure-mode audit on this slide. For each text element, tell me: is this Type 1 (redundancy — I'll say this aloud), Type 2 (seductive detail — interesting but not essential to the learning objective), or Type 3 (density — essential but arriving as too large a working-memory load)? Then rewrite the slide: headline as a full-sentence claim of twelve words or fewer, body as labeled bullets of eight words or fewer, notes field carrying the full explanation and any Background material.*

When you get the rewrite, count the body words on the repaired slide. Then ask yourself: which of the three types did your slide actually have? Most slides have all three. The exercise of naming the type per element is the teaching — it is what builds the diagnostic eye for the next slide, before the audit is needed.

---

**What would change my mind:** A controlled study comparing the three-failure-mode audit against naive word-count reduction in actual lectures, finding no measurable difference in retention. The three underlying principles are individually well-supported, but the claim that diagnosing the type before cutting matters more than simply cutting is mine, and it is not yet measured at the granularity I would want.

**Still puzzling:** I do not have a clean threshold for when density crosses from "this could be segmented" to "this belongs in a textbook chapter, not a slide at any density." The practitioner heuristic is roughly fifty words for a live-delivery slide; above seventy-five you have built a document. Those numbers are not empirical. They are calibrated from worked examples, and they may be wrong for disciplines where the structural claim requires a longer minimum encoding.

## Exercises

### Warm-up

**1.** A slide reads: "The French Revolution began in 1789 with the storming of the Bastille, a fortress-prison in Paris that had become a symbol of royal tyranny, and marked the end of the Ancien Régime, a social and political order that had governed France since the medieval period." Name the failure type or types present and state the correct fix for each.
*Tests: ability to identify Type 2 (seductive detail — Bastille history) and Type 3 (density — four structural facts in one sentence). Difficulty: low.*

**2.** A speaker delivers a ten-minute lecture on photosynthesis. Every sentence she speaks aloud appears verbatim on the slide behind her. A student in the front row is visibly reading the slide rather than watching the speaker. Name the failure type, name the cognitive mechanism causing the problem, and name the correct repair.
*Tests: Type 1 redundancy — verbal channel collision — move narration to notes field. Difficulty: low.*

**3.** Which of the following is the correct fix for a Type 3 (density) failure?
(a) Delete the excess text.
(b) Move the text to the notes field.
(c) Segment the text into labeled bullets of eight words or fewer, or split into multiple slides.
(d) Replace the text with an image.
*Tests: distinguishing the three operations; the answer is (c). Difficulty: low.*

### Application

**4.** Here is a slide title: "Causes of World War I." Rewrite it as a full-sentence headline claim of twelve words or fewer. Then explain in one sentence why your version is auditable and the original is not.
*Tests: label-vs-claim distinction; the ability to compress a structural assertion into a headline. Difficulty: medium.*

**5.** You are auditing a slide on machine learning overfitting. The body text reads: "Overfitting occurs when a model learns the training data too well, including its noise and random fluctuations, resulting in poor generalization to new data. The term was popularized in the 1990s as neural networks became more widely studied, though the underlying concept appears in earlier statistical literature." Identify the failure type for each sentence. State the correct operation for each. Then write the repaired slide body (headline + bullets only, no prose).
*Tests: applying the three-failure-mode audit to a two-sentence body; producing the repaired form. Difficulty: medium.*

**6.** A colleague argues: "I just keep all my slides under forty words and I never have text problems." Construct the strongest counterexample — a slide that is under forty words but still fails by one of the three failure modes. Name which type it fails and why word count alone cannot catch it.
*Tests: understanding that the three failure modes are mechanism-based, not count-based. Difficulty: medium.*

**7.** You paste a slide into an AI tool with the prompt "make this slide less text-heavy." The model returns a version with fewer words but still has the Bavarian-immigrants-style clause in the body. Explain why the model failed, using the vocabulary of this chapter, and rewrite the prompt so the model would have caught it.
*Tests: diagnosis of under-specified prompts; application of the three-failure-mode audit prompt. Difficulty: medium.*

### Synthesis

**8.** Take any slide from a deck you have built or been given. Run the three-failure-mode audit by hand: for each text element, assign it Type 1, Type 2, or Type 3 (it may have more than one). Then produce: (a) the repaired slide body with headline claim and labeled bullets; (b) the notes field text; (c) a one-paragraph audit log naming which type applied to each cut and why.
*Tests: full end-to-end application of the diagnostic and repair sequence. Difficulty: medium-high.*

**9.** The chapter claims that a headline makes a slide auditable. A colleague pushes back: "A good topic label is fine — experienced instructors know what the slide is about and can judge the content without a claim." Write a two-paragraph response that (a) steelmans your colleague's position and (b) explains why the auditability argument still holds even for experienced instructors.
*Tests: ability to hold the chapter's central claim under pressure while engaging honestly with the counterargument. Difficulty: high.*
